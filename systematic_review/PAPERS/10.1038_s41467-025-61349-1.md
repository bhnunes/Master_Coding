## Automated cell annotation and classi fi cation on histopathology for spatial biomarker discovery

Received: 17 December 2024

Accepted: 17 June 2025

Check for updates

Zhe Li 1,7 , Seyed Hossein Mirjahanmardi 1,7 , Rasoul Sali 1 , Feyisope Eweje 1 , Matthew Gopaulchan 1 , Leon Kloker 2 , Xiaoming Zhang 3 , Guoxin Li 4 , Yuming Jiang 1,5 &amp; Ruijiang Li 1,6

Histopathology with hematoxylin and eosin (H&amp;E) staining is routinely employed for clinical diagnoses. Single-cell analysis of histopathology provides a powerful tool for understanding the intricate cellular interactions underlying disease progression and therapeutic response. However, existing efforts are hampered by inef fi cient and error-prone human annotations. Here, we present an experimental and computational approach for automated cell annotation and classi fi cation on H&amp;E-stained images. Instead of human annotations, we use multiplexed immuno fl uorescence (mIF) to de fi ne cell types based on cell lineage protein markers. By co-registering H&amp;E images with mIF of the same tissue section at the single-cell level, we create a dataset of 1,127,252 cells with high-quality annotations on tissue microarray cores. A deep learning model combining self-supervised learning with domain adaptation is trained to classify four cell types on H&amp;E images with an overall accuracy of 86%-89%, and the cell classi fi cation model is applicable to whole slide images. Further, we show that spatial interactions among speci fi c immune cells in the tumor microenvironment are linked to patient survival and response to immune checkpoint inhibitors. Our work provides a scalable approach for single-cell analysis of standard histopathology and may enable discovery of novel spatial biomarkers for precision oncology.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&amp;E)-stained histopathology is a standard clinical procedure and is widely used for diagnosing diseases. Histopathology images mayalso provide valuable prognostic and predictive information that can be used to guide treatment decisions 1 -3 . However, the ability to extract useful information from complex large-scale histopathology images is rather limited with the traditional manual annotation approach.

Recent advances in arti fi cial intelligence (AI) have revolutionized biomarker discovery based on histopathology 4 -6 . Most pathology AI studies have adopted the patch or tile-based approach, where the whole-slide images are divided into many smaller tiles that can be fed into deep neural networks 7 -12 . A signi fi cant drawback of the tile approach is that it leads to black-box models that are dif fi cult to interpret, hindering their utility in high-stakes clinical applications.

Further, since each image tile contains dozens or hundreds of cells, the resultant models often lack direct connections to the underlying pathobiology.

Single-cell analysis of histopathology provides a powerful tool for understanding the complex cellular interactions that underlie disease progression and therapeutic response 13,14 . Several groups have explored single-cell-focused approaches to histopathology image analysis 15 -20 . However, such efforts have been hampered by errorprone human annotations. Manual annotation of cell types is inherently limited by inter-observer variability and the inability to distinguish cell types with subtle differences. Consequently, many studies have focused on tumor cells and lymphocytes that can be readily recognized in H&amp;E images 16 . For identifying macrophages (a common cell type in tumors), the inter-pathologist agreement is only around 50% 17 . Moreover, since manual annotation is a labor-intensive process, the number of annotated cells in previous work is often limited 15,19 , which may be insuf fi cient for training reliable deep learning models.

To overcome the limitations of manual annotations, emerging studies have utilized serially stained H&amp;E and immunohistochemistry (IHC) images for cell labeling 21 . There are several important shortcomings with prior work. Compared with immuno fl uorescence, IHC has limitations in terms of sensitivity, speci fi city, and protein quanti fi cation 22 . One study used H&amp;E and single-plex immunofl uorescence staining which creates signi fi cant challenges for accurate cell annotations and for training classi fi cation models 23 . In other studies, the cell types are con fi ned to cancer vs non-cancer cells with limited resolution of immune cells 24 . Finally, none of these studies performed rigorous validation of the cell classi fi cation models on diverse datasets to test their generalizability, or demonstrated clinical relevance of H&amp;E-based single-cell analysis for predicting treatment outcomes.

In this study, we develop an experimental and computational framework for automated cell annotation and classi fi cation on H&amp;E histopathology images. Brie fl y, we perform serial multiplexed immuno fl uorescence (mIF) and H&amp;E staining of the same tissue section. The mIF images provide a means to obtain an accurate de fi nition of cell types based on cell lineage protein markers. We then co-register H&amp;E with mIF images at the single-cell level to establish the ground truth of cell type labels on H&amp;E, which are used to train a deep learning model for classifying four major cell types in the tumor microenvironment. To improve the model ' s generalizability and reduce the impact of potential staining variations across different institutions, we integrate a self-supervised learning strategy with domain adaptation techniques into the classi fi cation model. The performance of the cell classi fi cation model is evaluated on both tissue microarray and whole-slide images, which signi fi cantly outperforms models trained with manual cell annotations. Further, we show that spatial interaction between speci fi c immune cells is linked to clinical outcomes including response to immunotherapy in cancer patients.

## Results

## Study design

We developed an experimental and computational approach for automated cell annotation and classi fi cation on H&amp;E-stained histopathology images without human annotations (Fig. 1). To establish reliable ground truths for cell types, we performed serial multiplexed immunofl uorescence (mIF) staining of antibodies to protein markers on formalin- fi xed paraf fi n-embedded (FFPE) tumor samples followed by H&amp;E staining of the same tissue section (Fig. 1a). The mIF imaging included 5 cell lineage protein markers (pan-CK, CD3, CD20, CD66b, CD68), which were used to de fi ne 4 cell types commonly found in the tumor microenvironment: tumor cells, lymphocytes, neutrophils, and macrophages (Fig. 1b). We then performed co-registration between the mIF and H&amp;E-stained images and ensured accurate alignment at the single-cell level. Through this process, we created a dataset of

1,127,252 cells with high-quality annotations based on their cell lineage markers in a high-throughput manner and then transferred the cell type labels to corresponding cells on the H&amp;E-stained images, therefore eliminating the need for error-prone human annotations (Fig. 1c). It is important to note that mIF is required solely for training the cell classi fi cation model and only standard H&amp;E images are needed for downstream applications.

To develop a robust cell classi fi cation model, we combined selfsupervised learning using 1,127,563 unlabeled cells extracted from an independent dataset of H&amp;E images and supervised learning using our mIF-labeled dataset to train a deep learning model for classifying cells on H&amp;E images (Fig. 1d). We evaluated generalizability of the cell classi fi cation model on 9 external validation datasets including a tissue microarray (TMA) with 136 cores and 3263 whole-slide images (WSIs). Finally, we performed single-cell analysis on 12 independent cohorts of 3605 patients across 8 common cancer types (Fig. 1e) and identi fi ed novel spatial biomarkers of prognosis and response to immunotherapy (Fig. 1f, g).

## Multiplexed immuno fl uorescence imaging for cell type identi fi cation

We conducted mIF imaging experiment on two FFPE tumor sections, which contain a total of 180 tissue microarray (TMA) cores obtained from 94 treatment-naïve gastric cancer patients (i.e., 1st TMA). Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are described in Supplementary Fig. 1. The mIF staining panel on the fi rst section included fi ve protein markers: CD3, CD20, pan-CK, PD1, and Foxp3, while the second panel included another fi ve markers: CD66b, CD68, CD8a, PD-L1, and CD163. Supplementary Fig. 2 shows a few sample mIF images obtained from the two panels. In this study, we used cell lineage markers (i.e., pan-CK, CD3, CD20, CD66b, CD68) to de fi ne four major cell types in the tumor microenvironment: tumor cells, lymphocytes, neutrophils, and macrophages. The other markers such as PD1 are related to cell function and were not used in this study.

To de fi ne cell types based on mIF images, we used an unsupervised clustering approach rather than manual gating that relies on somewhat arbitrary cutoff values. Speci fi cally, we performed the Leiden clustering algorithm 25 on the protein marker intensity values and cell nucleus areas in the two mIF panels separately. The cells in the fi rst panel were clustered into four groups, while the cells in the second panel were clustered into three groups (Fig. 2a, c). The cell clusters are well separated from each other, and each cluster has a distinct marker expression pro fi le (Fig. 2b, d). For example, cell cluster 1 in the fi rst panel had high expression of pan-CK and low expression of CD3 and CD20, which was de fi ned as tumor cells. Similarly, macrophages and neutrophils were identi fi ed based on the marker expression of CD68 and CD66b on the second panel, respectively. The distribution of cell nucleus size for different cell types is shown in Fig. 2e. These results demonstrate that the mIF images allow for reliable cell type identi fi cation based on the cell lineage marker expression pro fi les. In total, our approach resulted in 969,387 individual cells assigned to one of the four cell types by mIF in the 1 st TMA.

## Image co-registration for transferring cell type labels from mIF to H&amp;E

Since the mIF and H&amp;E images were acquired separately using different experimental protocols, they should be spatially aligned fi rst before downstream analysis. Importantly, the accuracy of co-registration mustbeatthesingle-cell level, which is essential to reliably transfer cell types de fi ned on mIF to H&amp;E images. To do this, the process of image registration was initiated with core-level image registration and subsequently re fi ned to cell-level registration. Speci fi cally, we applied an initial rigid transformation 26 using keypoint detection and matching algorithms to establish an approximate alignment between paired mIF and H&amp;E images. To enhance positional information of nuclei, preprocessing steps included normalization and contrast adjustment, improving visibility of nuclei and key structures. Following this, we employed non-rigid registration methods using gradient-based optimization 27 , which allowed for fi ner alignment by adjusting local variations and deformations at multiple pyramid levels. This approach ensured precise cell-level registration across different regions of the tissue. All co-registered cores were visually inspected and veri fi ed by pathologists to ensure accuracy and reliability of the alignment.

## Cell classi fi cation in H&amp;E images using deep learning

We randomly divided the entire annotated single-cell H&amp;E image dataset from 1 st TMAbypatients into two groups: training set (460,953 cells from 60% patients), and internal validation set (361,850 cells from 40% patients). Additionally, we performed the same experiment of serial mIF and H&amp;E staining on the same tissue section using a new set of TMA sections consisting of 136 cores of 112 gastric cancer patients from Southern Medical University (SMU). For the 2 nd TMA, we processed the images using the same procedure as above and obtained 304,449cellswith ground truth labels on H&amp;E images. This dataset was used for external validation of the cell classi fi cation model. Since T and B cells exhibit similar cell morphology based on H&amp;E images, we merged them into a single cell type, i.e., lymphocyte. The number of cells for each cell type in each dataset is shown in Supplementary Table 2.

Initially, we employed self-supervised learning to train a classi fi cation model that extracts robust cell presentations. Subsequently, we applied a deep domain adaptation technique using an independent dataset of 1,127,563 unlabeled cells extracted from H&amp;E images, combining domain adaptation and deep feature learning into a single training process. This approach further enhanced the model ' s generalizability. The performance of the cell classi fi cation modelinthetraining, internal validation, and external validation sets is shown in Fig. 4a, b. Across all three datasets, the model consistently achieved a high area under the curve (AUC) for one-vs-rest comparison (average AUC: 0.980 for training set, 0.972 for internal validation set, 0.964 for external validation set). Similarly, based on the confusion matrix, the model also achieved reasonably high accuracy for cell classi fi cation (overall accuracy: 91.3% for training set, 89.1% for internal validation set, 86.4% for external validation set). Within the four cell types, the model shows relatively higher performance in classifying lymphocytes and neutrophils (average accuracy: 91.8% and 90.1%).

This transformed every single cell into a point in a 2D space that reveals the similarity between cells within the same type and differences in cells across different types. We found that tumor cells and neutrophils are mapped to opposite locations and are well separated in the UMAP space, con fi rming a high degree of distinction in their morphological patterns. Next, we compared our automated cell annotation with traditional manual annotation approach. In particular, when training the model on a smaller subset of our dataset -consisting of 117,976 cells with the same distribution as the manually annotated dataset -it still signi fi cantly outperformed the model trained using manual annotations, which performed poorly with an overall accuracy of only 56.2% for cell classi fi cation (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). One disadvantage of Hover-Net is that it detected fewer cells than StarDist (284,965 vs 304,449) in our dataset.

## Concordance with gene expression-based estimates of cell type composition

We further evaluated generalizability of the cell classi fi cation model using whole-slide images (WSIs) across 8 common tumor types including lung, breast, esophageal, stomach, colon, rectum, liver, and pancreas cancers in the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). To do this, we applied the locked cell classi fi cation model trained from the 1 st TMAto 3263 H&amp;E-stained WSIs for 3112 patients in TCGA and computed the cell density for each of the four cell types. We then applied established bioinformatics algorithms to calculate the estimated density of four cell types (tumor cell, lymphocyte, macrophage, and neutrophil) by using RNA-seq data for each tumor. We evaluated the concordance between image-based cell classi fi cation results and gene expressionbased estimates. It is worth noting that this result is affected by differing measurement platforms (gene expression vs. image-based) as well as tumor heterogeneity given that RNA sequencing and H&amp;E staining were performed on different tumor sections; therefore, the correlation is not expected to be particularly high. Nonetheless, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 9, our automated cell annotation approach achieved substantially higher correlation to gene expression-based cell estimates compared with manual annotation approach across all 8 cancer types in TCGA (mean Pearson correlation: automated: 0.61 vs manual: 0.11).

## Cell-cell spatial proximity measures linked to patient prognosis

To demonstrate its clinical relevance, we applied the cell classi fi cation model to H&amp;E-stained images and obtained a single-cell map consisting of four cell types in the tumor microenvironment (TME). For each tumor, we quanti fi ed the cell density, cell composition, and cell-cell spatial proximity and distribution among the four cell types.

We then investigated prognostic relevance of spatial cellular features across 10 independent cohorts (2 gastric TMA cohorts and 8 TCGA cohorts) with a total of 3297 patients across 8 cancer types. Several spatial features were found to be signi fi cantly associated with prognosis after adjusting for multiple testing (Supplementary Tables 5 -14). Among these, one promising feature is the fraction of neutrophils among 50 nearest neighboring cells of a lymphocyte. As showninFig. 5, this spatial feature was strongly associated with overall survival across 8 common cancer types in all 10 cohorts (range of hazard ratio: 1.91 to 5.75; all P &lt;0.003). In multivariable Cox regression analysis, the lymphocyte-neutrophil interaction feature was independent of age, gender, disease stage, and tumor grade across all 10 patient cohorts, suggesting it could further add prognostic value beyond traditional clinicopathologic risk factors (Supplementary Tables 15 -24).

Finally, we investigated the impact of cell classi fi cation on prognostic performance. We replaced the proposed model with the second-best cell classi fi cation model DINO+ViT and repeated the prognostic analysis. As a result, we observed a reduction in prognostic performance compared with the original model (HR = 2.69, P =0.003, vs. HR=1.84, P =0.06) in the 2 nd TMA (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 10). This indicates that accurate cell classi fi cation is crucial for ensuring prognostic performance.

## Single-cell analysis identi fi ed spatial biomarker of immunotherapy response

We analyzed digitized H&amp;E whole-slide images as well as clinical and outcomes data for two independent cohorts of advanced gastroesophageal patients treated with anti-PD-(L)1 immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). The same cell classi fi cation model and spatial feature extraction procedure were applied to both patient cohorts to develop models for predicting objective response and progression-free survival (PFS). To build a predictive model for PFS, we employed a stepwise feature selection process. First, we removed redundant features by calculating the variance in fl ation factor (VIF) 36 to detect multicollinearity among features. Second, we performed univariate Cox regression analysis, selecting a subset of features associated with PFS ( P &lt;0.1). Finally, we built a multivariate Cox regression model, regularized using the elastic net 37 , with optimal regularization parameters determined through grid search with fi ve-fold cross-validation. For predicting objective response, we used logistic regression with elastic net and included all 66 features as input. Similar to the PFS model, we determined optimal hyperparameters using grid search and crossvalidation.

For predicting objective response, four spatial features were included in the fi nal model: average amount of lymphocytes within 50 microns of a tumor cell, fraction of lymphocytes among 50 nearest neighboring cells of a macrophage, fraction of tumor cells among 50 nearest neighboring cells of a macrophage, and average amount of cells within 50 microns of a tumor cell. Their feature weights in the model are 0.18, 0.17, -0.05, and 0.03, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 11). The model achieved AUC = 0.832 (95% CI: 0.724 -0.940) in the discovery cohort and AUC = 0.817 (95% CI: 0.752 -0.881) in the validation cohort, signi fi cantly outperforming the clinical biomarker PD-L1 combinedpositive score (CPS) with AUC = 0.650 (95% CI: 0.578 -0.722) in the validation cohort (Fig. 6a, b). For both cohorts, the spatial biomarker shows a more than 2-fold increase in sensitivity compared to CPS at 90% speci fi city (Fig. 6c).

For predicting PFS, four spatial features were included in the fi nal model(SupplementaryFig. 12). In the discovery cohort, patients with a higher biomarker value had a signi fi cantly worse PFS compared to patients with a lower biomarker value, with a hazard ratio = 3.16 (95% CI 1.83 -5.46), P &lt;0.001 (Fig. 6d). In the external validation cohort, the spatial biomarker positive population had signi fi cantly worse PFS compared to the spatial biomarker negative population, with a hazard ratio = 2.32 (95% CI 1.54-3.49), P &lt;0.001.

Importantly, the spatial biomarker strati fi ed patients for PFS across different subgroups as de fi ned by PD-L1 CPS, lines of immunotherapy, and treatment regimens such as ICI monotherapy or chemo-ICI combination therapy (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 13). The better strati fi cation in patients treated with ICI monotherapy suggests that the model is predictive of ICI outcomes speci fi cally. Further, the spatial biomarker also strati fi ed patients for PFS within molecularly de fi ned subgroups such as microsatellite stable and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) negative tumors. ## Discussion

In this study, we present an experimental and computational approach for automated cell annotation and classi fi cation on standard histopathology images. By co-registering mIF with serially stained H&amp;E images, we created a dataset of 1,127,252 cells with cell type labels de fi ned based on the expression of cell lineage protein markers. We then trained a deep learning model that achieved an overall accuracy of 86%-89% to classify four cell types on H&amp;E images. Single-cell histopathology analysis can reveal valuable insight into the complex cellular interactions in the tumor microenvironment. However, the ability to identify different cell types in histopathology images is limited by the inef fi cient and error-prone human annotations 17 . Although their approach for spatial cellular analysis 17 is similar to ours, we have shown that the accuracy of cell classi fi cation is crucial in downstream applications, e.g., for ensuring prognostic performance of the spatial biomarkers.

Producing high-quality, large-scale cell annotations has been a challenging problem. The traditionalapproach of manual annotation is inherently limited by inter-observer variability and human ' s inability to distinguish cell types with visually subtle differences. For instance, the inter-pathologist agreement was 0.63 (Cohen ' s kappa) in the Lizard dataset 38 and for macrophage was only ~50% (Pearson correlation) in ref. 17. By contrast, our automated cell annotation approach uses cell lineage protein marker expression as the ground truth for cell labels. Therefore, it can produce high-quality cell annotations at scale, reducing the subjectivity and variability of manual annotation.

In this study, we use mIF imaging to guide cell type identi fi cation and transfer the cell type labels to H&amp;E images via single-cell spatial registration, therefore eliminating the need for error-prone human annotations. Another key advantage of our approach is scalability.

Ourworkisdistinct from previous studies that used deep learning to generate virtual staining images from unstained, H&amp;E stained or IHC-stained images 39 -42 . Instead of aiming for image generation on a pixel basis, we focus on the fundamental biological unit of living organisms, i.e., a cell. The goal of our work is to learn intrinsic image phenotypes of a cell and explicitly classify them into different cell types. This enabled us to obtain a detailed single-cell spatial map from routinely available H&amp;E images, which can be directly used for the discovery of biologically relevant cellular features linked to patient outcomes.

In our study, we found that spatial interactions among speci fi c immune cells in the tumor microenvironment are associated with survival in cancer patients. For instance, patients whose lymphocytes are in closer proximity to neutrophils in their tumors had signi fi cantly worse survival. This suggests that neutrophils may play an immunosuppressive role in the tumor microenvironment, which is consistent with previous fi ndings 43 . The spatial proximity of lymphocytes to neutrophils could be indicative of an altered immune response that is less effective at eliminating cancer cells or controlling disease progression, leading to poor patient outcome. A recent study shows that spatial cellular patterns predict clinical bene fi t for immune checkpoint inhibitors 16 . The spatial analysis is limited to cancer cells and tumorin fi ltrating lymphocytes. Investigation of additional cell types such as macrophages and neutrophils in the tumor microenvironment should further enhance the predictive power of spatial analysis.

The tumor microenvironment is a complex ecosystem that contains many more cell types beyond the four most common cell types investigated in our study. As a proof of concept, we de fi ned cell types using traditional mIF imaging that has a limited multiplexing capability (typically up to 5). Recent development of highly multiplexed imaging technology such as co-detection by indexing 44 or one-shot high-plex immuno fl uorescence 45 , may be used to identify additional cell types on the same tissue section as the H&amp;E. Additionally, while traditional spatial transcriptomics only provide spotlevel information and cannot resolve single-cell data, there are emerging methods 46 that measure gene expression at sub-cellular resolution, which can also be used to de fi ne more cell types than mIF. Leveraging these advanced spatial molecular pro fi ling technologies can increase the cellular resolution and provide deeper biological insight into the tumor microenvironment.

In summary, we present an experimental and computational framework for automated cell annotation and classi fi cation on standard H&amp;E-stained images without human annotations. We demonstrate the applications for quanti fi cation of cell composition and cell-cell interactions in the tumor microenvironment. Given the routine use of H&amp;E slides, our approach is applicable in broad patient populations and may be used for discovery of novel spatial biomarkers for precision oncology.

## Methods

## Patients and tumor specimens

We collected and analyzed data for a total of 3,605 patients across 8 cancer types from 12 independent cohorts. This retrospective study was approved by the institutional review board at Stanford University (IRB protocol number 67432) and Southern Medical University (SMU) (IRB protocol number NFEC2017171). Informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians, as applicable, in accordance with institutional guidelines. The details of the patient cohorts and associated tumor samples are described below.

Tissue microarray (TMA) cohorts . Formalin- fi xed and paraf fi nembedded (FFPE) tumor specimens were collected from 94 treatment-naïve patients (59 male and 35 female; median [IQR] age: 70[59 -77]) who underwent surgical resection for gastric cancer. TMA sections were constructed by Shanghai Outdo Biotech (SOB) using one or two core samples per patient with 1.8 mm diameter cores and 4 um thickness, resulting in a total of 180 cores. The cell classi fi cation model was developed using the TMA sections obtained from SOB (i.e., 1 st TMA). For external validation of the cell classi fi cation model, we constructed another set of TMAs with 136 cores using FFPE tumor samples obtained from 112 treatment-naïve patients (80 male and 32 female; median [IQR] age: 61[51 -67]) who underwent resection for gastric cancer at SMU (i.e., 2 nd TMA). Clinical data including demographics, tumor stage, and follow-up information for overall survival were collected for patients in both cohorts (Supplementary Table 25-26).

TCGA cohorts . We collected and analyzed 3263 publicly available H&amp;E-stained whole-slide images (WSIs) for 3112 patients (1176 male and 1842 female, 94 unknown; median [IQR] age: 62[53 -71]) of eight cancer types in TCGA, i.e., esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), rectum adenocarcinoma (READ), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), Lung Adenocarcinoma (LUAD), and Breast Invasive Carcinoma (BRCA). Clinical information including demographic data, tumor stage, RNA-seq data, and follow-up information for overall survival was collected from the Genomic Data Commons.

Immunotherapy cohorts . We performed single-cell analysis of pretreatment H&amp;E-stained WSIs and evaluated the ability to predict response and survival outcomes of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in two independent cohorts of gastroesophageal cancer patients. The discovery cohort includes 98 patients (66 male and 32 female; median [IQR] age: 64 [53 -73]) with advanced gastroesophageal cancer (originating from the esophagus, gastroesophageal junction, or stomach) treated with ICIs at Stanford University. The external validation cohort includes 189 patients (108 male and 81 female; median [IQR] age: 56 [48 -65]) with advanced gastric adenocarcinoma treated with ICIs at SMU. Clinical data including age, gender, tumor differentiation, CPS, MSI status, and HER2 status were collected from the electronic health record. Treatment outcomes were assessed using objective response and PFS. Patients were divided into two groups: responders (complete or partial response) and non-responders (stable or progressive disease). PFS was determined from the date of treatment initiation until the date of progression or death; patients who did not progress were censored at the date of the last follow-up. Baseline characteristics of patients in the two Immunotherapy cohorts are summarized in Supplementary Tables 27, 28.

## Multiplexed immuno fl uorescence staining and image processing

For both TMA cohorts, multiplexed immuno fl uorescence (mIF) staining was performed on two consecutive TMA sections using two panels of antibodies: panel 1, pan-cytokeratin (CK), CD3, CD20, PD1, and Foxp3; panel 2, CD66b, CD68, CD8a, PD-L1, and CD163. Information about the antibody source and staining conditions is summarized in Supplementary Table 29. All markers were stained in sequence using their respective fl uorophore contained in the Opal 7 IHC kit. Positive controls with human reactive tonsils and negative or auto fl uorescence controls were included in each run of staining. Here, we used mIF instead of multiplex IHC because of several advantages, particularly in terms of sensitivity, multiplexing, localization, quanti fi cation, and speci fi city, which allow better de fi nition of cell types 22 .

The mIF-stained TMA slides were scanned using the multispectral microscope Leica imaging system under fl uorescence conditions at 10x magni fi cation. The mIF images were visually checked for staining quality and artifacts by a pathologist (A.A. with 17 years of experience in clinical and molecular pathology). 177 cores from 94 patients of 1 st TMAand136coresfrom 112 patients of 2 nd TMApassed quality control and were used in this study. Single-cell analysis of mIF images were performed using the InForm 2.4.0 digital image analysis software (Akoya Biosciences). Cells were segmented into nucleus and cytoplasm compartments based on which the mean and maximum values of marker expression were computed for every cell.

## Cell type identi fi cation on mIF images

We initially segmented a total of 2,103,400 cells based on in the fi rst TMA panel. Each cell is associated with the expression values of fi ve protein markers. In this study, we used cell lineage markers (i.e., CD3, CD20, and pan-CK) to de fi ne cell types. On each mIF core, the cell expression matrix consists of the mean expression value of a cell for three markers and its area including nucleus and cytoplasm compartments in square microns. Outliers (cells with a very large size &gt;99.7th percentile) were removed 47 . The mean expression value for all markers within the cells were normalized by Z-score. For cell type identi fi cation, we concatenated the expression matrices of all TMA cores and performed Leiden algorithm in an iterative manner for clustering and annotation 45,48,49 . In each iteration, we plotted radar chart and expression heatmaps, and assigned a cluster to a speci fi c cell type only when it exhibits high expression of their associated lineage markers and low expression for all other markers. For the clusters that cannot be annotated, we repeated the above steps until no additional clusters with clear enrichment expression were generated. We applied the same procedure to process cells in the second TMA panel with a total of 2,189,911 cells, and used cell lineage markers CD66b and CD68 to identify neutrophils and macrophages.

## H&amp;E staining and image processing

Following the mIF experiment, H&amp;E staining was performed on the sameTMAsections after removal of fl uorescent dyes. The H&amp;E-stained slides were digitized using the Olympus scanner at 40x magni fi cation (~0.272 μ m/pixel). Except for the two TMA datasets, all other datasets (8 TCGA and 2 ICI cohorts) included digitized H&amp;E-stained whole-slide images(WSIs). For TCGA datasets, nearlyall slides were scanned at 40× magni fi cation (with only 115 slides scanned at 20×) using Aperio scanner and all images were used. For the ICI datasets, all slides were scanned at 40× magni fi cation using Philips or Aperio scanners. To minimize the in fl uence of image artifacts, we removed those regions with pen marks, folding and blurring artifacts by using different color fi lters. We applied color normalization to H&amp;E images to mitigate stain variability across institutions. For cell detection and segmentation in H&amp;E images, we applied a pretrained deep convolutional neural network-StarDist 34 which achieved the top position in the CoNIC 2022 challenge 50 for segmenting all cell nuclei in H&amp;E images.

## Co-registration of mIF and H&amp;E images

We extracted all the TMA cores from each panel and conducted the spatial co-registration on each core individually. Speci fi cally, we performed image registration with an initial coarse core-level procedure and advanced to a more detailed cell-level registration. First, we preprocessed both mIF and H&amp;E images using channel-wise normalization, grayscale conversion, and contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization 51 to enhance the positional information of nuclei. Then, we used the Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT) 52 for keypoint detection and descriptor computation, followed by rigid transformations using the SuperGlue algorithm 26 . This process is done iteratively to evaluate multiple transformations at different levels to fi nd the optimal transformation between mIF and H&amp;E images. Subsequently, we performed non-rigid image registration using the DeeperHistReg algorithm 27 , which involves creating image pyramids, initializing or resampling the transformation, and iteratively optimizing it at each pyramid level using gradient-based methods. Finally, the co-registered images were visually inspected and veri fi ed to ensure their quality and accuracy.

To evaluate the accuracy of the co-registration process, we computed the Euclidean distance between every cell on the H&amp;E image and its nearest neighbor cell on mIF image. The distance is then compared with the diameter of a nucleus obtained by the segmentation algorithm. Additionally, we compared the average distance of cells with the average size in every single core individually and plotted the distributions for all cores on both panels.

## Cell type de fi nition on H&amp;E images

To de fi ne the cell types on H&amp;E images, we used the cell labels obtained from mIF data. Each cell on co-registered H&amp;E images was assigned to the label for its matching pair, i.e., cell with the nearest distance on the mIF images. The label was assigned only if the cell-cell distance was less than 5 microns, which is about the average radius of a nucleus. For cell classi fi cation purposes, we included only those cells that were identi fi ed on mIF as one of the four cell types: tumor cells, lymphocytes, macrophages, and neutrophils. To further validate our automated cell annotation, we compared with manual annotation on a subset of the H&amp;E images. Speci fi cally, we randomly selected 10 cores from the 1st TMA. A board-certi fi ed pathologist (XZ) annotated tumor cells and lymphocytes on each core, and we computed the Cohen ' s kappa with 95% con fi dence intervals. We did not compare macrophages or neutrophils due to the known limitations of manual annotations which can result in unreliable ground truth 17,38 .

## Supervised learning with domain adaptation for cell classi fi cation

To train the cell classi fi cation model, we segmented and extracted all cell nuclei in image patches, where each patch contains a single cell with a dimension of 75 × 75 pixels. To remove the impact of surrounding cells, the background was rendered white with a mask, facilitating the model ' s focus on the target cell. Since cell segmentation and classi fi cation was performed separately, we can easily merge different datasets (e.g. 1 st TMA, 2 nd TMA, Lizard, and NuCLS) to create a larger training dataset with more diverse cell types, enabling the development of a more robust and generalizable classi fi cation model. In contrast, models like CellViT 53 struggle to handle such heterogeneous, merged datasets due to their requirements on training data.

In this study, we combined supervised training with selfsupervised learning on single-cell images (rather than 224 × 224 image patches) and domain adaptation to improve generalizability of the cell classi fi cation model. Speci fi cally, we fi rst detected the tumor areas in 372 WSIs from a multi-institutional cohort of 106 patients in the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium Colon Adenocarcinoma (CPTAC-COAD) dataset. Then, we sampled two tumor patches with a size of 2048 × 2048 from each WSI and used StarDist to segment cell nuclei in the sampled patches, resulting in 1,127,563 cells de fi ned by D .

When deploying the model on new H&amp;E images, cells with all predicted probabilities &lt;50% for the four cell types (tumor cells, lymphocytes, neutrophils, and macrophages) are categorized as ' Others ' . This approach ensures that cells that do not con fi dently fall into any of the four prede fi ned categories are not misclassi fi ed as one of these types. Additionally, we have reported the proportion of cells from the four target classes in both the internal and external validation sets that received probabilities below 50% (Supplementary Table 30).

To further validate this strategy, we generated two extended datasets. Speci fi cally, we selected the top fi ve TMA cores on 1 st TMA and 2 nd TMAwhere the appearance and cell count of their consecutive tissue sections were highly similar. Note that not all TMA cores can be used for de fi ning ' Others ' as the mIF staining is performed on consecutive sections with slight differences in cell appearances. We then co-registered the H&amp;E image on Panel 1 with both the mIF image on Panel 1 and the mIF image on Panel 2 for these cores. This coregistration allowed us to transfer the cell labels (tumor cells, lymphocytes, neutrophils, and macrophages) from the mIF images to the H&amp;E image on Panel 1, identifying cells that did not fall into any of the four categories, thereby generating a set of cells belonging to the ' Others ' category. Through this process, 47,096 cells for 1st TMA and 31,632 cells for 2nd TMA were de fi ned as ' Others ' .

We then merged these newly identi fi ed ' Others ' cells with the original training dataset from the 1 st TMA to create a merged training dataset and repeated this process on the 2 nd TMA to generate the merged validation dataset. The updated model was trained with these merged datasets, resulting in a 5-class model. Supplementary Fig. 16 compares the performance of the original 4-class model and the newly trained 5-class model for classifying 5 cell types including ' Others ' . The results demonstrate that the 4-class model shows very similar overall performance and is slightly better for classifying the 4 main cell types than the 5-class classi fi cation model, con fi rming the effectiveness of our inference strategy.

To rigorously validate our proposed framework, encompassing cell annotation and model development, we conducted comprehensive comparative experiments. First, we evaluated BYOL+ ResNet used in our proposed model against state-of-the-art self-supervised methods and foundation models, including VOLTA 29 , DINO+ResNet 30 , DINO+ViT 31 , UNI 32 , and Virchow 33 . We followed the training and implementation settings described in the original publication for VOLTA. We implemented DINO+ResNet and DINO+ViT using the default settings in the solo-learn Python package (https://github.com/vturrisi/solo-learn). For a fair comparison, we pretrained VOLTA, DINO+ResNet,andDINO+ViTonthesame 1,127,563 cells (75×75 patches) as BYOL + ResNet. For UNI and Virchow, we resized the cell images to 224×224 before inputting them into the models. To fi ne tune them, we froze their backbones except for the last block, which was trained along with the classi fi er head.

## Comparison with the manual annotation approach

To compare our automated cell annotation with manual annotation, we incorporated two of the largest publicly available datasets: Lizard 38 and NuCLS 18 . Since the Lizard dataset does not contain annotations for tumor cells or macrophages, we extracted lymphocytes and neutrophils only. Similarly, we obtained tumor cells, macrophages, and neutrophils from the NuCLS dataset. To construct a more balanced manually annotated dataset, we identi fi ed and included additional tumor cells, macrophages, and neutrophils from NuCLS that were originally provided with only bounding box annotations. For these cells, we applied Stardist segmentation in combination with the available bounding boxes to generate high-quality cell segmentations. We then merged these images to create a training set of 117,976 manually annotated cells, comprising 92,006 lymphocytes, 4095 neutrophils, 1338 macrophages and 20,537 tumor cells. To reduce batch effects caused by differences in staining and imaging protocols between Lizard and NuCLS, we applied stain color normalization to harmonize color distribution across datasets. Additional techniques, including domain adaptation, dataset resampling, and extensive brightness and color augmentation, were implemented during model training to further address class imbalance and batch effects. We randomly divided this manually annotated singlecell dataset into a training set (60%) and an internal validation set (40%). As both public datasets contain only H&amp;E images and lack mIF images of the same section, no automated cell annotations were available for them. We used the same model architecture and training techniques, including self-supervised learning and domain adaptation, to train a cell classi fi cation model based on the manually annotated datasets. We trained three different models: one with the manually annotated datasets, one with a subset of the automated cell annotation dataset matched in size and distribution to the manually annotated dataset, and one with the full automated cell annotation dataset. To ensure comparability, all three models were evaluated on the same external validation set, which consisted of 304,449 cells with ground truth labels on H&amp;E images from the 2 nd TMA.

## Single-cell spatial feature analysis

For single-cell spatial analysis, we quanti fi ed the cell composition and cell-cell interactions among the four cell types as determined by the cell classi fi cation model on H&amp;E images. The implementation details of features are described below:

## Cell composition

Fraction: The fraction of each cell type among all 4 cell types

Density: The number of each cell type and the overall number of cells per unit area

Area: The area of each cell type and the overall cell area

## Cell-cell interaction

Spatial proximity: The minimum distance between cells of type 1 to cells of type 2

Spatial distribution: The number of cells of type 1 within 50 microns around cells of type 2

Cell neighborhoods: The fraction of cells of type 1 among 50 nearest neighbors around cells of type 2

Cell-cell interaction features were computed in a pairwise fashion by iterating all possible cell pairs resulting in 16 features for each category. These features were computed for each cell and then averaged over all cells of the same type. For TMA cores, we did single-cell analysis on the entire image for each patient. For WSIs, we randomly sampled eight image patches with a size of 2048×2048 at 40x magni fi cation from the tumor area. After calculating the features for each patch, we averaged them over all patches for each patient. In total, we computed 66 features to quantify cell composition and cell-cell spatial interactions in the tumor microenvironment.

## Statistics and reproducibility

We used Kaplan -Meier analysis to assess the relation between individual single-cell features and overall survival in all cohorts. Patients were strati fi ed into two groups based on the median or optimal value of the computed features. The log-rank test was used to compare the two groups with low and high feature values. P values were corrected for multiple hypothesis testing using the Bonferroni correction. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically signi fi cant.
