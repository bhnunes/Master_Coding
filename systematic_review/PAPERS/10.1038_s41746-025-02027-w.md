The complexity and variability of high-resolution pathological images present signi fi cant challenges in computational pathology. While AI-driven pathology foundation models have advanced the fi eld, they require large-scale datasets, substantial storage, and signi fi cant computational resources, as well as rigorous validation for clinical applicability. We present PathOrchestra, a versatile pathology foundation model trained on 287,424 slides from 21 tissue types across three centers. Evaluated on 112 tasks from 61 private and 51 public datasets, covering digital slide preprocessing, pan-cancer classi fi cation, lesion identi fi cation, multi-cancer subtype classi fi cation, biomarker assessment, gene expression prediction, and structured report generation. Across 27,755 whole slide images and 9,415,729 region-of-interest images, it achieved over 0.950 accuracy in 47 tasks, including pancancer classi fi cation, lymphoma subtyping, and bladder cancer screening. It is the fi rst to generate structured reports for colorectal cancer and lymphoma. Overall, PathOrchestra demonstrates the clinical readiness of large-scale self-supervised pathology foundation models, achieving high accuracy and offering potential to digital medicine integration.

Pathology is heralded as the gold standard for disease diagnosis, encompassing a broad range of tasks including tumor detection 1,2 , typing 3,4 , grading 5,6 , molecular expression analysis 7 -10 , prognosis assessment 11,12 , and prediction of treatment response 13 -15 . These tasks require handling highresolution and morphologically diverse pathological images, posing signi fi cant diagnostic resource challenges. Computational pathology (CPath) leverages powerful algorithms to interpret and quantify pathological images, providing a more precise understanding of disease mechanisms. However, traditional AI training paradigms relying on large annotated datasets face critical limitations, as accurately labeling vast pathological data across diverse disease types is often impractical, which underscores the urgent need for generalizable models that can ef fi ciently handle multicenter data while reducing reliance on manual annotation.

The advent of self-supervised learning in computer vision (e.g., DINOv2 16 , MoCov3 17 , MAE 18 ) and the success of large language models like the GPT series 19,20 have propelled the development of data-driven pretraining methodologies. These models 21 -24 leverage vast collections of whole slide images (WSIs) to learn high-quality feature representations from unlabeled data, demonstrating state-of-the-art performance in downstream tasks using limited labeled data and fewer training parameters. Despite these advancements, a comprehensive evaluation of foundational pathology models is still lacking. Furthermore, the impact of pre-training data and model architecture on performance in downstream tasks remains underexplored. While technical assessments have been carried out, the clinical applicability and potential of these models remain insuf fi ciently addressed, limiting their full integration into real-world clinical settings.

## PathOrchestra: a comprehensive foundation model for computational pathology with over 100 diverse clinicalgrade tasks

<!-- image -->

In this paper, we present PathOrchestra (Fig. 1), a pathology foundation model that sets new performance benchmarks through a rigorous evaluation across 112 clinically relevant downstream tasks. PathOrchestra is implemented as a self-supervised vision encoder, pretrained on an extensive corpus of 287,424 hematoxylin and eosin (H&amp;E)-stained whole-slide images encompassing 21 distinct tissues, organs, and systems, sourced from both proprietary archives and publicly available datasets from three independent centers. We systematically examine its translational potential in diverse clinical scenarios (Fig. 2), demonstrating robust and consistent performance on a large-scale evaluation comprising 27,755 WSIs and 9,415,729 region-of-interest (ROI) images (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). PathOrchestra achieved an accuracy exceeding 0.950 in 47 tasks, thereby establishing state-of-the-art benchmarks in real-world applications, including digital slide preprocessing, pan-cancer classi fi cation, lesion identi fi cation and detection, multi-cancer subtype classi fi cation, biomarker assessment,gene expression prediction, and structured pathology reporting.

## Results

## Dataset curation

The training dataset consists of 287,424 WSIs, covering 21 distinct types of tissues, organs, and systems, including Lymph Node (68,054), Stomach (59,598), Breast (32,890), Cervix (27,786), Lung (26,842), Bladder (16,326), Prostate (10,188), Kidney (9,328), Brain (7,240), Blood System (4,232), Colon (4,088), Liver (3,344), Uterus (3,086), Ovary (2,962), Skin (2,550), Head and Neck (2,526), Esophagus (2,492), Bone (1,890), Pancreas (932), Nerve (770), and Eye (300). These WSIs were digitized from frozen and formalin- fi xed paraf fi n-embedded (FFPE) tissue slides stained with H&amp;E. The dataset consists of 268,859 WSIs derived from FFPE samples and 18,565 from frozen samples, resulting in a FFPE-to-frozen sample ratio of ~14:1. The scanning process utilized equipment from four different manufacturers, including the SQS-600P scanner (Shenzhen Shengqiang Technology Co., Ltd., China), using a 20 × objective and generating images in .sdpc format; the KF-PRO-005 scanner (Ningbo Jiangfeng Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd., China), employing a 40 × objective and producing .kfb fi les; the Aperio ScanScope GT 450 scanner (Aperio, USA), also using a 40 × objective to generate .svs fi les; and the Pannoramic MIDI II scanner (3DHISTECH, Hungary), utilizing a 40 × objective and producing .mdsx fi les. We sampled256 × 256 patches at 20 × magni fi cation, generating a total of 141,471,591 patches, all of which were used in the actual training.

The training dataset includes data from three sources, including the Xijing Hospital, Fourth Military Medical University, Xi ' an, China (20232024) with 123,169 WSIs and 13,303 cases; The First Af fi liated Hospital of USTC, Hefei, China (2022-2024) with 133,999 WSIs and 16,749 cases; and TheCancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), USA (2006-2016), which encompasses contributions from Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center, Johns Hopkins University, University of California Los Angeles (UCLA), University of Michigan, Mayo Clinic, University of Washington, and University of Texas MDAnderson Cancer Center, totaling 30,256 WSIs and 10,990 cases. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Xijing Hospital (IRB No. KY20252335) and The First Af fi liated Hospital of USTC (IRB No. 2025-RE-204) for the retrospective analysis of internal pathological images. Prior to computational analysis and model development, all internal data including WSIs and pathology reports were de-identi fi ed to ensure patient con fi dentiality. Since the study involved retrospective analysis of archival pathological slides without direct patient recruitment, informed consent was not required. The training data consisted solely of pathological images without any associated patient diagnoses or personal identi fi ers. Details were shown in Supplementary Table 4.

## Pathology image preprocessing and quality control tasks

We classi fi ed the preprocessing tasks in CPath into two main categories which are pathology quality control tasks and general analysis tasks. The quality control tasks include wrinkle detection, bubble and adhesive identi fi cation, contamination detection, and blur detection. The general analysis tasks encompass pathology image identi fi cation, recognition of H&amp;E and IHC staining, identi fi cation of IHC marker types, magni fi cation recognition, differentiation between frozen and FFPE sections, identi fi cation of biopsy versus large specimens, and recognition of IHC membrane and nuclear positivity. Preprocessing is a critical step in the CPath work fl ow, directly impacting the performance of subsequent algorithms. Developing a feature extractor with strong generalization capabilities can signi fi cantly enhance model performance while reducing reliance on extensive training data and annotations. All preprocessing tasks were conducted using supervised ROI classi fi cation based on the linear probing method, with model performance evaluated using ACC and F1 scores. Detailed information on the preprocessing tasks and experimental setups is provided in the Methods section.

Among the 12 preprocessing-related tasks (Fig. 3), the PathOrchestra model achieved ACC and F1 scores exceeding 0.950 in 7 subtasks. The general analysis tasks demonstrated superior performance compared to the quality control tasks, with ACC and F1 scores surpassing 0.970 for pathology image identi fi cation, recognition of H&amp;E and IHC staining, recognition of H&amp;E and fl uorescence staining, magni fi cation discrimination, and differentiation between frozen and FFPE sections. In quality control tasks such as bubble and adhesive identi fi cation, the model achieved ACC and F1 scores above 0.980. These results demonstrate the model ' s robust capability to effectively execute a wide range of preprocessing tasks without extensive retraining.

## Pan-cancer classi fi cation tasks

We evaluated PathOrchestra on slide-level pan-cancer classi fi cation tasks, including a 17-class pan-cancer tissue classi fi cation using an in-house FFPE dataset, a 32-class classi fi cation using data from the TCGA FFPE dataset, anda32-class classi fi cation using data from the TCGA frozen tissue dataset. These tasks assessed the model ' s performance across different cancer types, data sources, and specimen preparation methods. Pan-cancer classi fi cation models are designed to recognize and distinguish multiple cancer types, necessitating strong generalization capabilities crucial for the early detection of various cancers. All tasks were performed using weakly supervised WSI classi fi cation based on the attention-based ABMIL, with model performance evaluated using AUC, ACC and F1 scores. Detailed information on these tasks and experimental setups is provided in the Methods section.

In the 17-class pan-cancer tissue classi fi cation task (Fig. 4a), the model achieved an average value for AUC of 0.988, ACC of 0.879, and F1 score of 0.863. Notably, the Prostate classi fi cation task achieved perfect scores (ACC, AUC, and F1 = 1.0). Upon inspection, all prostate samples were needle biopsies, which have consistent and visually distinctive features compared to other organ types that are mostly large surgical specimens. We speculate that this uniform sampling modality contributes to the model ' s strong performance in this task. For the 32-class classi fi cation using the TCGA FFPE dataset, the model attained the AUC of 0.964, ACC of 0.666, and F1 score of 0.667 (Fig. 4b). We observed that the classi fi cation performance for frozen sections was 1.4% lower in AUC, 8.9% lower in ACC, and 9% lower in F1 compared to FFPE sections under similar conditions (Fig. 4c). This discrepancy may be attributed to the better preservation of tissue structure and morphology in FFPE sections, which facilitates feature extraction and classi fi cation. These results underscore the model ' s robust generalization capabilities in pan-cancer classi fi cation tasks, highlighting its potential as a foundational pathology model for screening multiple cancer types and improving diagnostic accuracy.

## Multi-cancer subtype classi fi cation tasks

Tumor classi fi cation and subtyping are critical in enhancing diagnostic accuracy and personalizing treatment plans, thereby optimizing patient prognosis and clinical outcomes. We evaluated the performance of PathOrchestra across tumor classi fi cation and subtyping tasks, including cervical cancer subtyping, bladder cancer screening (WSI and ROI), cervical TCT screening (WSI and ROI), gastrointestinal benign and malignant screening, lymphoma and reactive hyperplasia screening, lymphoma cell origin classi fi cation, colorectal cancer detection, cervical epithelial lesion detection, colorectal and lung tissue screening (LC25K), lung cancer subtyping (LC25K-lung), colorectal subtyping (LC25K-colon), breast cancer screening (BreastHis), lung cancer subtyping (TCGA-NSCLC), colorectal subtyping (TCGA-RCC), lymphoma subtyping, breast cancer subtyping (BACH), esophageal cancer screening (TCGA-ESCA), colorectal tissue classi fi cation (HunCRC-patch), prostate ISUP grading (PANDA), prostate Gleason grading, colorectal high and low-grade screening, gastric cancer subtyping (PatchGastricAD22), prostate cancer tissue classi fi cation (AGGC), glioma IDH1 screening (TCGA-IDH1), multi-organ lymph node metastasis screening, colorectal polyp screening, colorectal subtyping (HunCRC-WSI), renal cancer tissue classi fi cation (TCGA-RCC), colorectal tissue classi fi cation (CRC-100K), colorectal subtyping (institutional data), colorectal tissue classi fi cation (Kather), and brain subtyping (Ebrains). Detailed information on these tasks and experimental setups is provided in the Methods section.

In the evaluation of 34 tumor classi fi cation and subtype identi fi cation tasks (Fig. 6), PathOrchestra demonstrated broad adaptability and high performance levels. Notably, in the classi fi cation tasks for cervical and bladder cancers, PathOrchestra excelled. For instance, the ACC for benign and malignant bladder cancer classi fi cation reached 0.954, with an F1 score of 0.943 and an AUC value of 0.989, showcasing the model ' s exceptional performance in this task. Furthermore, in the patch classi fi cation task for bladder cancer, the model ' s F1 score improved to 0.945, while the ACC and AUC values also reached 0.945 and 0.988, respectively, indicating that the model maintains ef fi ciency even in more granular image processing scenarios. In the screening for benign and malignant gastrointestinal tumors, PathOrchestra exhibited remarkable precision, achieving an ACC of 0.993, anF1scoreof0.976,andanAUCashighas0.988.Theseresultshighlightthe model ' s strong diagnostic potential in the detection and classi fi cation of gastrointestinal cancers. In lymphoma-related classi fi cation tasks, PathOrchestra also demonstrated robust performance. For example, in the task of classifying lymphoma versus reactive hyperplasia, the model achieved an ACC of 0.975, an F1 score of 0.941, and an AUC of 0.994. In the more re fi ned classi fi cation of B/T-cell lymphomas, PathOrchestra recorded an F1 score of 0.918 and an AUC of 0.993, re fl ecting the model ' s high accuracy in subtype classi fi cation. Similarly, in the subtype classi fi cation tasks for colorectal and lung cancers, PathOrchestra performed admirably. In the colorectal subtype classi fi cation (LC25K-2), all evaluation metrics (ACC, F1, and AUC) achieved 1.000, demonstrating absolute classi fi cation accuracy. Likewise, for the lung cancer subtype classi fi cation (LC25K-3), the model attained a high ACC and F1 score of 0.999, with an AUC of 0.999, indicating strong generalization capabilities across different tissue types.

However, despite PathOrchestra ' s outstanding performance in most tasks, there are areas for improvement. For instance, in the IDH1 screening task for gliomas, although the AUC reached 0.940, the accuracy and F1 scores were relatively low at 0.896 and 0.894, suggesting that the model ' s precision in screening this speci fi c subtype may not match its performance in other cancers. In more complex tasks, PathOrchestra ' s performance was comparatively weaker. For example, in the prostate cancer grading (AGGC) task, the model achieved an ACC of only 0.660, an F1 score of 0.828, and an AUC of 0.873, re fl ecting limitations in handling highly complex pathological tasks. Additionally, in glioma screening (Ebrains), although the AUC reached 0.871, the ACC and F1 scores were notably lower at 0.386 and 0.278, indicating a need for further optimization in brain tumor screening. In summary, PathOrchestra demonstrated exceptional accuracy and robustness across various cancer classi fi cation and subtype identi fi cation tasks, particularly excelling in bladder cancer, gastrointestinal cancers, and colorectal cancer tasks. Nevertheless, there remains room for improvement in certain complex tasks, such as prostate cancer grading and glioma screening. This indicates that while PathOrchestra performs excellently in most tasks, further optimization is necessary for speci fi c tasks to enhance its overall diagnostic capability.

## Biomarker assessment tasks

Biomarker evaluation is critical for clinical diagnosis, treatment selection, and prognostic assessment. We evaluated PathOrchestra across 36 biomarker-related tasks involving the qualitative assessment and scoring of commonlyusedIHCmarkers.Theseincludedover30proteinmarkerswith varied biological functions, expression patterns, and clinical signi fi cance. The model demonstrated robust performance across diverse markers and datasets, highlighting its adaptability to complex molecular evaluation tasks. Full task descriptions and experimental details are provided in the Methods section.

PathOrchestra achieved varying levels of performance across the 36 tasks as shown in Fig. 7. High accuracy was observed in markers such as HER2 scoring and CD20 qualitative analysis, with performance exceeding 0.920. For more complex tasks involving combinations of IHC markers, accuracy ranged from 0.800 to 0.880. The model showed promising capability in analyzing nuanced histopathological features, with markers like PD-L1 and CyclinD1 yielding strong results. In contrast, tasks involving subtle expression differences, such as CD56 and CXCL-13, were more diffi cult, with accuracies around 0.700. These results suggest the need for further model re fi nement and more comprehensive training datasets. Evaluation of hormone receptor-related biomarkers, including ER and PR, also showed solid performance, with accuracies around 0.900. Overall, these fi ndings demonstrate the strong potential of PathOrchestra for biomarker evaluation, while also indicating areas where future improvements are needed.

## Structured report generation tasks

Generating pathology reports is one of the most challenging clinical tasks in computational pathology, primarily due to the large number of WSIs per patient, the complexity of diagnostic content, and the dif fi culty in predicting subtasks. By selecting appropriate disease types and breaking down the pathology report into a structured format, we can use arti fi cial intelligence algorithms to generate pathology reports from diagnostic and auxiliary examination perspectives. This approach can effectively save time of pathologists while providing reasonable diagnostic explanations, thus holding signi fi cant value for clinical practice. This study focuses on two diseases, lymphoma and colorectal cancer, employing a subtask combination method to generate structured reports for each patient based on the PathOrchestra model.

In the task of determining lymphoma subtypes, conventional HE image diagnoses often do not provide suf fi cient evidence to identify speci fi c subtypes, usually requiring quantitative analysis from IHC, with different subtype suspected patients corresponding to different immunohistochemical panels. This paper emphasizes the AITL and DLBCL subtypes, combining the HE subtype prediction task with the qualitative assessment of 29 IHC markers, and diagnosing multiple differently stained pathology slides for each patient, resulting in structured reports. As seen in example from Fig. 9a, the model demonstrates the ability to diagnose lymphoma subtypes and provides detailed diagnostic explanations via immunohistochemistry. For instance, in Patient 1, the algorithm incorrectly predicted CD21 as negative, and due to data loss during the collection process, it was unable to provide effective diagnoses for CD20, CXCL-13, and CD10, while the qualitative assessments of the other IHC markers were consistent with the actual diagnoses by pathologists. The results of subtype diagnosis and accompanying IHC reports indicate that our model is capable of making comprehensive diagnoses for this complex disease.

## Methods

## Network architecture and pretraining protocol

Toachieve robust performance across 112 clinical-grade downstream tasks, we pre-trained our model on the curated dataset using the DINOv2 architecture 16 , which is designed to extract high-quality visual features through self-supervised learning on unlabeled data. DINOv2 employs a teacher-student framework based on Vision Transformers (ViT) 26 , leveraging an alignment mechanism that enables the student network to learn meaningful feature representations without relying on labeled data. By eliminating the dependence on manual annotations, DINOv2 allows for the extraction of rich features from large-scale unlabeled images, signi fi cantly reducing the need for human intervention. The architecture enhances feature generalization through multi-scale, multi-view data augmentation techniques. By incorporating image crops at different scales and applying diverse augmentations, the model learns feature representations that are robust and generalize well across various downstream tasks, including classi fi cation, detection, and segmentation.

Weadopt the pretraining strategy of DINOv2 to develop a pathologyoriented visual foundation model on a large-scale unlabeled histopathology dataset. DINOv2 employs a teacher-student framework trained via multiview self-distillation. Each training image is augmented into a set of global and local crops. The global views encourage the model to capture high-level tissue architecture, while the local crops focus on fi ne-grained features such as cellular morphology and nuclear atypia. A key component of DINOv2 is its implicit sample selection mechanism: only a subset of local views is selected to compute the loss against the global teacher outputs, avoiding explicit negative sampling. This contrastive-like design is well-suited for histopathology, where structural hierarchies are consistent within images but highly variable across samples. This strategy avoids reliance on negative sampling while encouraging the student network to align with meaningful and diverse representations from different spatial scales and contexts. Furthermore, all image crops undergo standardized data augmentation including random cropping, color jittering, grayscale conversion, Gaussian blurring, and horizontal fl ipping. These augmentations simulate domainspeci fi c variations such as differences in staining protocols, section thickness, and scanner types. The combination of multi-scale view sampling and strong augmentation enables the model to focus on structure-dominated features, improving the robustness and generalizability of learned representations across diverse pathology sources.

The training process of DINOv2 primarily consists of the following three steps. The fi rst step is data preprocessing and augmentation, which involves applying various augmentations such as random cropping, horizontal fl ipping, color jittering, grayscale conversion, and Gaussian blur to the original images. These operations generate multiple global and local crops, which are simultaneously fed into the student network for feature extraction. The second step is feature extraction and alignment. The student network extracts features from both global and local crops, while the teacher network extracts features solely from the global crops. The DINO and iBOT heads are then used to compute the alignment loss between the features extracted by the student and teacher networks. The third step involves computing the loss and updating the network. The total loss is calculated using the DINO 27 loss, iBOT 28 loss, and KoLeo regularization 29 , after which the student network ' s parameters are updated through backpropagation. Simultaneously, the teacher network ' s parameters are updated using the Exponential Moving Average (EMA) mechanism 30 , ensuring that the teacher network remains smoother and more stable. In the experiments conducted in this paper, we chose to use the teacher network as the encoder for downstream tasks. This decision is grounded in the observation that the teacher network, which is updated through EMA, exhibits smoother and more stable parameters. Consequently, this leads to enhanced generalization capabilities. Therefore, the encoder of the teacher network is more adept at handling downstream tasks that demand high robustness and applicability across different domains.

## Clinical task assessment methodology

Weevaluatedthepre-trainedmodelonadiversesetof112clinicallyrelevant downstream tasks, including pathology quality control, pan-cancer classifi cation, lesion detection, multi-cancer subtyping, biomarker assessment, gene expression prediction, and structured report generation.

Pathology Image Preprocessing and Quality Control Tasks. In clinical pathology, image quality is crucial for the accuracy of the fi nal diagnosis. Image preprocessing eliminates factors that affect image quality, such as focus blur, tissue folding, bubbles, and impurities, ensuring clarity and diagnostic value. Quality control tasks automate image screening to exclude low-quality images that may compromise subsequent analysis. This approach signi fi cantly reduces manual intervention, enhancing pathologists ' ef fi ciency and diagnostic accuracy. To assess the model ' s ability to handle common artifacts and variations in pathological images, we conducted several quality control tasks focusing on image quality assessment and recognition of staining methods, magni fi cations, and specimen types. For image quality assessment, we developed classi fi ers to detect various issues that can impede accurate pathological analysis. These issues included differentiating between natural images (sourced from the ImageNet-1K validation set) and pathological images (random patches from gastrointestinal H&amp;E and immunohistochemistry data), identifying tissue folds, bubbles, glue artifacts, contaminants such as dust, hair, and fi bers, and detecting blurriness using the FocusPath-UofT dataset 31 . In terms of staining and specimen recognition, we evaluated the model ' s capability to recognize different staining methods, magni fi cations, and specimen types. We classi fi ed images based on staining type, distinguishing between H&amp;E and IHC stained images, as well as between H&amp;E stained images and immuno fl uorescence images from the DeepCell dataset 32 . We also classi fi ed four types of IHC markers (PR, ER, Ki67, and HER2) to assist in molecular characterization, differentiating IHC markers based on nuclear (PR, ER, Ki67) and membrane (HER2) positivity. Furthermore, we developed models to distinguish images at different magni fi cations (10 × and 20 ×) to improve model robustness and to identify whether H&amp;E images were from frozen sections or FFPE samples. Specimen type recognition included classifying images based on specimen size to enhance preprocessing work fl ows. For these tasks, we utilized datasets comprising thousands to hundreds of thousands of images, employing linear probing strategies.

Pan-cancerClassi fi cation Tasks. Pan-cancerclassi fi cation tasks involve uni fi ed modeling of image data from different cancer types to assist pathologists in quickly identifying the cancer type during pathological examination, preventing misdiagnosis due to incorrect cancer type assumptions. Clinically, pathology departments often face cases where the cancer origin is unclear, especially during initial assessments when pathologists may not be able to immediately identify the cancer type. By training a pan-cancer classi fi cation model, the system can provide preliminary diagnostic suggestions, supporting early screening. Additionally, multi-cancer classi fi cation improves the recognition of rare cancers, enhancing the model ' s generalization ability in cases of data scarcity. To assess the model ' s ability to distinguish between major cancer types across organs, we de fi ned a set of pan-cancer classi fi cation tasks, which involve coarse-grained, multi-class classi fi cation at the cancer-type level. These tasks simulate real-world diagnostic scenarios where slides from different organs are mixed, and prior knowledge of cancer type may be unavailable. Such modelscanassist in initial triage, cross-organ screening, and error detection. We implemented three pan-cancer tasks: (1) a 17-class classi fi cation task using 1071 WSIs from an in-house dataset spanning cancers of the lung, kidney, breast, prostate, and other organs. (2) a 32-class FFPE pan-cancer classi fi cation task based on 3,036 WSIs from TCGA. (3) a 32-class frozen pan-cancer classi fi cation task based on 3,038 TCGA WSIs. All tasks were formulated using the weakly supervised ABMIL framework 33 with consistent training parameters to ensure comparability. These pan-cancer tasks are distinct from cancer subtyping tasks, which focus on fi ner-grained classi fi cation within a single cancer type (e.g., lung adenocarcinoma vs. squamouscellcarcinoma).Incontrast,pan-cancerclassi fi cation emphasizes organ-level differentiation and bene fi ts from shared representation learning across diverse cancer types.

Multi-organ Lesion Identi fi cation and Analysis Tasks. The core of this task is to automatically identify and analyze lesion areas in pathology slides using deep learning techniques. This technology has broad clinical applications, particularly in the early detection and accurate localization of tumors. For pathology images from different organs, the model can identify lesion regions within seconds and automatically segment potential tumor tissue. Pathologists can use these analyses for further diagnosis, improving diagnostic ef fi ciency and accuracy while reducing the risk of missed or incorrect diagnoses. We assessed the model ' s capability in lesion detection and segmentation across multiple organs and modalities. For lesion detection, we trained models to detect breast cancer metastasis in lymph node WSIs using datasets from the CAMELYON16 and CAMELYON17 challenges. We developed models to segment regions for PD-L1 assessment in lymphoma, enhancing immunotherapy decision-making, and classi fi ed images based on the presence of tumor-in fi ltrating lymphocytes using the TCGA-TILs dataset. Additionally, we performed binary classi fi cation of metastatic cancer in breast tumor regions using the PCam dataset, which contains over 2 million image patches, and identi fi ed signet ring cell carcinoma in gastrointestinal sections using the DigestPath dataset. Training strategies included linear probing and fi ne-tuning, with images processed at appropriate magni fi cations. For segmentation tasks, we employed the Mask2Former model 25 , fi ne-tuned with a Vision Transformer Adapter (ViT-Adapter) 34 , to segment eight major cell types within tumor tissues using the SegPath dataset, segment glands in colorectal adenocarcinoma tissue sections using the GlaS dataset, and segment nuclei across 19 tissue types using the PanNuke dataset. We also utilized the ViTDet model 35 for few-shot detection tasks, detecting and classifying positive cells in cervical cell pathology ROIs with over 1.3 million images and identifying abnormal cells in pleural and peritoneal fl uid pathology slides using 36,544 ROI images.

Multi-cancerSubtypeClassi fi cation Tasks. We de fi ne cancer subtyping tasks as those aimed at capturing fi ner-grained distinctions within a single cancer type, including histological subtypes, tumor grades, and benignmalignant classi fi cation. These distinctions are crucial for tailoring treatment strategies and making accurate prognostic assessments, ultimately improving treatment outcomes and patient survival. We evaluated the model ' s capacity to perform both binary and multi-class classi fi cation across diverse organs. Binary classi fi cation tasks included distinguishing cancer frombenigntissuesin bladder, cervical, and digestive tract cancers, as well as differentiating lymphoma from reactive hyperplasia and B-cell from T-cell lymphomas. For multi-class subtyping, we conducted organ-speci fi c classi fi cation tasks: detecting cervical precancerous lesions using the TissueNet dataset, categorizing tissue types with LC25K subsets, and classifying benign vs. malignant breast tumors at high magni fi cation using BreaKHis. We further subtyped non-small cell lung cancer and renal cell carcinoma using TCGA data, performed detailed lymphoma subtyping to guide treatment, graded prostate cancer using core needle biopsies and Gleason scores, and identi fi ed gastric adenocarcinoma subtypes using the PatchGastricADC22 dataset. Model training employed both linear probing and the ABMIL framework to optimize classi fi cation accuracy.

Biomarker Assessment Tasks. We assessed the model ' s ability to evaluate the expression of biomarkers critical for diagnosis and treatment planning, particularly in the context of IHC. Biomarkers play a crucial role in tumor subtyping, prognostic assessment, and treatment decisionmaking. The expression of each biomarker re fl ects the biological characteristics of the tumor, which in turn in fl uences clinical decisions. For example, HER2, ER, and PR markers are critical for targeted therapy in breast cancer. By automating the evaluation of these biomarkers, we signi fi cantly enhance diagnostic ef fi ciency and reduce human interpretation bias, especially in cases where marker expression is low or the samples are complex. The selection of biomarkers was based on their established clinical signi fi cance in tumor diagnosis, subtype classi fi cation, prognostic assessment, and treatment decisions. We performed multi-IHC marker quali fi cation by assessing the expression of 30 different IHC markers in lymphomas using 3,976 WSIs. These markers were chosen for their pivotal role in identifying lymphoma subtypes, evaluating prognosis, and guiding treatment decisions. Speci fi c marker analysis was conducted for markers such as CD5, CD3, CD20, CD79a, CD21, EBER, CD10, Bcl-6, Bcl-2, MUM1, CD4, CD23, PD-1, Cyclin D1, CD19, CD22, CD8, C-myc, CD56, Granzyme B, TIA-1, Perforin, CD2, CD30, CD7, CD38, ICOS, CXCL-13, ALK, PD-L1, HER2, ER, PR, Ki67, and others. These markers were selected for their known utility in lymphoma diagnosis, immunophenotype characterization, and prognostic value. In addition, we evaluated breast cancer biomarkers by assessing HER2 scoring and ER, PR, and Ki67 status, all of which are critical for breast cancer subtyping and treatment planning. Each task involved datasets ranging from 11 to 313 WSIs, ensuring robust and diverse evaluations. For all tasks, we consistently employed a weakly supervised classi fi cation method using the ABMIL framework with standardized parameters, ensuring consistency and reproducibility across all evaluations.

Gene Expression Prediction Tasks. Gene expression data is crucial for analyzing the molecular characteristics of cancers and for enabling precision treatment. By predicting gene expression, the model can provide pathologists with molecular-level diagnostic information, complementing traditional histological examination. This task holds signi fi cant potential in the molecular diagnosis of tumors and the identi fi cation of drug targets. To evaluate the model ' s ability to predict gene expression levels from pathological images using the HEST-Benchmark dataset 10 . We predicted expression levels of the top 50 genes across nine cancer types, including Invasive Ductal Carcinoma (IDC), Prostate Adenocarcinoma (PRAD), Skin Cutaneous Melanoma (SKCM), Colonic Adenocarcinoma (COAD), Rectal Adenocarcinoma (READ), Clear Cell Renal Cell Carcinoma (ccRCC), Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC), Lung Adenocarcinoma (LUAD), and Axillary Lymph Nodes in IDC (LYMPH\_IDC). By integrating and summarizing these tasks according to their major categories, we provide a cohesive overview of our evaluation of the pre-trained model across a diverse range of clinically relevant applications. This approach emphasizes the model ' s versatility and potential impact in various domains of pathology. Detailed descriptions of each task, including datasets, experimental setups, and training parameters, are available in the Supplementary Information for further reference.

Structured Report Generation Tasks. We explored the potential of directly applying vision foundation models for structured report generation. In clinical practice, generating reports for gastrointestinal cancer is complex, primarily involving the diagnostic classi fi cation and qualitative assessment of multiple slides. AI has the potential to assist pathologists in generating structured reports, thereby improving work ef fi ciency. Similarly, lymphoma, with its numerous subtypes and IHC markers, presents signi fi cant complexity in both subtype diagnosis and immunohistochemical evaluation. Moreover, regional disparities in healthcare resources exacerbate development imbalances. Compared to traditional report generation methods, we opted for a structured approach, as it provides more accurate and reliable results. In this study, we utilized the PathOrchestra model to generate structured reports for each patient, combining multiple task subsets. For lymphoma report generation, we fi rst analyze conventional H&amp;E stained images to diagnose lymphoma subtypes. However, H&amp;E staining often fails to de fi nitively identify speci fi c subtypes, necessitating the integration of IHC for more precise analysis. Different lymphoma subtypes correspond to distinct IHC panels. This study focuses particularly on diagnosing Angioimmunoblastic T-cell Lymphoma (AITL) and Diffuse Large B-Cell Lymphoma (DLBCL), combining H&amp;E subtype prediction tasks with qualitative assessments of 29 IHC markers. We employ weakly supervised learning to diagnose H&amp;E stained pathology slides and conduct qualitative analyses for each IHC type, ultimately generating the structured reports. In colorectal cancer, we focus on distinguishing tumor from nontumor tissues through H&amp;E images and classifying cancer grades

(cancerous, low-grade, high-grade). For non-malignant samples, we further categorize polyp types, including hyperplastic polyps, in fl ammatory polyps, polypoid hyperplasia, and cases with no polyps. For each patient, we utilize weakly supervised learning to train classi fi cation models for various tasks, resulting in the generation of structured reports.

## Evaluation protocol

Given that PathOrchestra is a patch-level vision foundation model, we adopted different evaluation strategies depending on the label granularity of each downstream task. For tasks with patch-level or region-level supervision, we applied linear probing on frozen features to evaluate the discriminative capacity of the encoder. For whole-slide classi fi cation tasks with only slide-level labels, such as biomarker expression assessment, we employed weakly supervised learning using the ABMIL framework to aggregate patch-level features into slide-level representations. This protocol ensures a consistent evaluation of the model across both localized and global supervision settings, following practices from previous foundation models such as UNI. For segmentation tasks, we employed the widely used Mask2Former architecture as a decoder on top of the frozen patch-level features extracted by the PathOrchestra encoder. The features were arranged into spatial grids and passed to Mask2Former, which was trained using standard cross-entropy loss to predict pixel-wise labels. This setup enables effective evaluation of the encoder ' s adaptability to dense prediction tasks while keeping the encoder fi xed. For ROI-level cell detection tasks, we adopted a transformer-based detection head following the ViTDet framework, which operates on frozen patch-level features extracted from each ROI and predicts bounding boxes and class labels. This setup allows accurate localization of cellular instances without modifying the backbone encoder. For region-level clustering tasks, we used a simple KNN approach on the extracted features to evaluate the structural separation of phenotypes in the learned embedding space.

Weadoptedarange of metrics to evaluate classi fi cation, detection, and segmentation tasks. For classi fi cation tasks, we reported balanced accuracy (ACC), weighted F1 score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Balanced accuracy measures the average recall across classes to address class imbalance. The weighted F1 score integrates precision and recall, weighted by class frequency. AUC re fl ects the model ' s capability to discriminate between classes over varying thresholds. For cell detection tasks, we used mean average precision (mAP), which summarizes detection performance across multiple con fi dence thresholds. For segmentation tasks, we reported class pixel accuracy (CPA), intersection over union (IoU), and Dice coef fi cient. CPA re fl ects per-class pixel accuracy. IoU measures the overlap between predicted masks and ground truth. Dice coef fi cient evaluates overall segmentation agreement. Detailed experimental setups and task-speci fi c results are provided in the Results section.

The ground truth for each clinical task was derived from different sources depending on the task type, including expert annotations, pathology reports, image metadata, and publicly available dataset labels. A comprehensive summary of the ground truth sources and corresponding label formats is provided in Supplementary Table 2. Model performance was quantitatively evaluated by comparing predictions to the corresponding ground truth labels for each task using standard metrics.

## Hyperparameters for pretraining

The pretraining of the PathOrchestra model was carried out using the following hyperparameters, optimized for large-scale computational pathology tasks. The model architecture comprises 24 layers, with 16 attention heads and a patch size of 16. The feed-forward network (FFN) utilizes a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) structure, with an embedding dimension of 1024. Stochastic dropout is applied at a rate of 0.1 to prevent over fi tting. For data augmentation, global crop scales range from 0.48 to 1.0, with a crop number and size of 2 at 224. Local crop scales range from 0.16 to 0.48, with 8 crops of size 96. The maximum masking ratio is set at 0.5, and the minimum masking ratio is set at 0.1, optimizing the model ' s robustness. Gradient clipping is applied with a max norm of 3.0, and normalization is performedon the last layer. The training employed a batch size of 3072 with the learning rate starting at 0 and increasing to 2e-3 after warmup. The learning rate gradually decays to 1e-6. The model also utilizes a teacher model with an initial temperature of 0.04, gradually increasing to 0.4, and a teacher momentum that transitions from 0.992 to 1.000. Training was conducted for 20 epochs, with warmup epochs set to 2, and teacher temperature warmup lasting for 6 epochs. The entire process is carried out using automatic mixed precision (FP16) to improve computational ef fi ciency. A detailed list of these hyperparameters is provided in Supplementary Table 3.
