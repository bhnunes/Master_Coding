Patients with High-Grade Serous Ovarian Cancer (HGSOC) exhibit varied responses to treatment, with 20 -30% showing de novo resistance to platinum-based chemotherapy. While hematoxylin-eosin (H&amp;E)-stained pathological slides are used for routine diagnosis of cancer type, they may also contain diagnostically useful information about treatment response. Our study demonstrates that combining H&amp;E-stained whole slide images (WSIs) with proteomic signatures using a multimodal deep learning framework signi fi cantly improves the prediction of platinum response in both discovery and validation cohorts. This method outperforms the Homologous Recombination De fi ciency (HRD) score in predicting platinum response and overall patient survival. Our study suggests that histology and proteomics contain complementary information about biological processes determining response to fi rst line platinum treatment in HGSOC. This integrative approach has the potential to improve personalized treatment and provide insights into the therapeutic vulnerabilities of HGSOC.

Debulking surgery followed by platinum-based chemotherapy has been the standard of care of HGSOC. Patients responding to fi rst-line platinumbased therapy often have a durable remission and the clinical bene fi t could also be further extended by the administration of targeted agents, such as Poly ADP-Ribose Polymerase (PARP) inhibitors. However, platinum resistance, de fi ned as disease recurrence within 6 months of fi rst-line platinum-based chemotherapy, occurs in approximately 25% of cases and the prognosis of such cases is worse with a median survival of 9 -12 months 1,2 . Patients with refractory disease experience the toxicity of platinum-based chemotherapy without bene fi t. Due to their rapid disease progression, such cases are commonly excluded from participating in clinical trials. Lack of mechanistic understanding signi fi cantly impedes developing treatment strategies to overcome fi rst line treatment resistance. Conversely, improved prediction models for treatment resistance could signi fi cantly enhance clinical decision-making, allowing for more effective treatment approaches that minimize exposure to potentially non-effective therapies and side effects, while ensuring that patients who are likely to respond to a treatment are identi fi ed.

Homologous recombination-de fi ciency (HRD) plays a major role in response to platinum-based treatments and PARP inhibitors. Cases with HRDcanbe identi fi ed using mutational signatures. Examples include nextgeneration sequencing-based methods including the HRD-score 3 , the FDAapproved methods (Myriad myChoice CDx 4 and FoundationFocus CDxBRCALOH 5 ), WGS-based HRDetect 6 , WES-retrained HRDetect 7 , and functional assays. (e.g. RAD51 foci). Although recent publications investigated the possibility of predicting HRD status using whole slide images 8,9 and predicting response to platinum-based treatments using WSI or proteomics data 10 -12 , their predictive power has not reached the level of clinical utility.

Investigating the proteome is informative of how tumors respond to platinum-basedtreatments. Recentstudies in proteogenomics have revealed that certain patterns of protein expression and modi fi cations are linked to patient survival rates and outcomes in cases of High-Grade Serous Ovarian Carcinoma (HGSOC) 11,13,14 . Additionally, Chowdhury et al. 10 described a signature of 64 proteins that can predict with high speci fi city which patients might develop resistance to initial platinum therapy. Recent advances in computational pathology demonstrate a potential for treatment response prediction using weakly supervised deep learning models. These computer vision algorithms can use Hematoxylin-Eosin (H&amp;E)-stained whole slide images (WSIs) to predict the outcomes of treatments like bevacizumab in ovarian cancer 15 . Additionally, these models can indicate HRD status 8,9,16 . This capacity to derive clinical insights directly from WSIs suggests that when combined with proteomic data, which offers a detailed molecular landscape of tumor behavior, we can signi fi cantly improve predictive models.

SinceWSI,proteomicsignaturesorDNArepairde fi ciencystatusareall predictive of various aspects of ovarian cancer biology, it is possible that their ' fusion ' may produce even superior predictive performance.

Early and late fusion models are two approaches in multimodal data fusion and analysis. Early fusion is similar to a multidisciplinary consultation where an oncologist, radiologist, and genetic counselor collaborate from the start, pooling their expertise to quickly form a comprehensive view of a patient ' s health. This approach is useful when the interplay among various medical data types is essential for accurate diagnosis and treatment planning. In contrast, late fusion is akin to sequential specialist consultations. Each expert examines the patient independently, contributing insights from their respective fi elds in detail and later integrate these fi ndings. This method allows for in-depth analysis of each data type, ensuring that unique insights are captured before they are synthesized into a diagnosis or treatment plan.

Whilethere are many upcoming fusion strategies such as intermediate, hierarchical and dynamic fusion, our study evaluates early and late fusion models by benchmarking recent methodological advances in multi-modal integration of histopathology and omics data 17 -21 . Wecomparethesemodels across metrics of performance and interpretability. Furthermore, multimodal fusion may provide opportunities for automatically identifying biomarkers related to treatment resistance. The way these models combine different modalities is designed to be inherently interpretable. Therefore, the integration of biological knowledge into the models facilitates detailed investigations at the patient level into the signi fi cance of speci fi c histopathology morphologies, pathways and proteins.

Our study is the fi rst to integrate proteomics and histopathological images and show that these modalities are synergistic for determining platinum response. Furthermore, we show that these models can be probed to provide fi ne-grained interpretability to better understand tumor response at a mechanistic level. (Fig. 1).

Next, we evaluated whether combining patient-paired H&amp;E WSIs and proteomics data using supervised multimodal deep learning frameworks could improve platinum response prediction. To this end, we benchmarked three multimodal model architectures: the PorpoiseMMF 19 model, a late fusion framework originally designed for integrative survival analysis and adapted to our task (Section: Multimodal Histopathology-proteomics Modeling), MCAT 21 an early fusion co-attention-based model (Section: Multimodal Histopathology-Proteomics Modeling); and SurvPath 20 , an early fusion model leveraging pathology and bioinformatics pathways data (Section: Multimodal Histopathology-Proteomics Modeling). These models were selected to represent a range of multimodal integration strategies.

For this multi-modal setup, we aimed to identify the optimal combination of model architecture (Section: Multimodal HistopathologyProteomics Modeling), proteomics feature selection strategy (Section:

Feature Extraction -Proteomics), and foundation model backbone for WSI patch embeddings (see the section Feature Extraction -Histopathology). These components were evaluated as independent and interchangeable variables.

Across all architectures, combining WSI and proteomics data resulted in a signi fi cant increase in performance compared to unimodal models (Supplementary Fig. 10b), which relied on WSI-only or proteomics-only inputs. However, we found that the con fi guration of a PorpoiseMMF 19 architecture, built on top of the Chowdhury proteomics signature (Methods Platinum-response Protein Signature ( ' Chowdhury Signature ' )) combined with the vision transformer backbone UNI 23 for H&amp;E patch embeddings (Section: Feature extraction -histopathology), provided the best performance. This model con fi guration outperformed other multimodal models across both primary and metastatic tumor analyses. For this reason, this model con fi guration is used going forward.

While late fusion showed slightly better performance over early fusion, modeling choices (H&amp;E image featurization model, proteomics grouping, multimodal architecture) may have complex effects on one another. For example, the choice of protein input and patch featurization model affects the number of model parameters. Our results indicate that a proteomics machine-learning-based feature selection followed by the simplest late fusion concatenation model PorpoiseMMF led to the overall most robust performance among all other architectures we studied. This may be due to the fact that late fusion facilitates a more tailored feature extraction and representation learning for each separate modality. With this, the model may leverage the strengths of each data source better handling disparities in the dimensionality, scale, and quality across modalities, leading to a more robust and accurate synthesis of information for decision-making. However, evidence from benchmarking suggests early fusion may outperform late fusion in the larger data regime. In the original SurvPath paper, models were benchmarked at over 1000 samples per class 20 , almost an order of magnitude larger scale than the available HGSOCresponse data we studied. Despite having lower AUC scores in our study, early fusion MCAT and SurvPath models have the advantage of enabling the in-depth cross-modality interpretability analysis we present. Such interpretability advantages may help comply with GDPR-related tests that need to take place before clinical deployment.

Although primary and metastatic cancers share many similarities, they exhibit signi fi cant differences in response. We found that training the models separately for the primary and metastatic tumors yields better overall model performance. Based on these fi ndings, we recommend the separation of WSIs originating from primary and metastatic tumors for studies modeling response.

## WSI + proteomics compared to genetics-based models

Loss of certain biological functions, such as the capacity of homologous recombination-based DNA repair, has been known to be associated with response to DNA damaging therapy in ovarian cancer 7 . Wecompared our WSI + proteomics-based model predictions against the predictive power of genomics-based methods of detecting homologous recombination de fi ciency status. The FDA-approved Myriad HRD-score assesses genomic alterations to indicate the presence of HRD. The FoundationOne CDx test offers comprehensive genomic pro fi ling, including TMB and genome-wide LOH(loss of heterozygosity) status, which correlates with HRD status. HR

Finally, complex classi fi ers, such as HRDetect 6 combine both large scale (MB size) and fi ner scale (single nucleotide mutational level, such as signature 3) mutational signatures extracted from wholegenome sequencing 27 .

## Interpreting neural network WSI based attention maps

As shown for the WSI in Fig. 4a, the unimodal WSI-only CLAM 22 allows us to visualize patch importances, or ' attentions, ' on the WSI, creating an ' attention heatmap ' (Fig. 4b). This visualization provides insight into relative importance of the tissue regions for the predictions. Patches with high attention are crucial for classifying the entire slide. For instance, if tumor areas of the WSI are highlighted on the attention map for a task that relies on known tumor localized histopathological features, it may validate that the model aligns with pathologists ' thinking. Additionally, when we lack documented histopathological features for a task, cohort-wide aggregated information from these heatmaps can help us discover important features the model considers signi fi cant. This is of particular interest for that task of predicting platinum response, where histopathology is not a primary biomarker for decision-making.

## Tumorregions inWSIattract high model attention and neighbors are frequently stroma

For the unimodal CLAM model we took the single highest attention patch from every WSI in the cohort and clustered this set of UNI 23 generated embeddings with K-means clustering. The closest patches to the k found centroids were annotated by tissue/cell type. We found that the 75% of these patches correspond to epithelial tumor cells (Supplementary Fig. 7). This suggests that tumor regions of WSIs gather high attention. This is an intuitive result as response of tumor should be strongly dictated by tumor cell morphology which re fl ects its behavior (See the section Interpretability Analysis for details).

While the critical role of the tumor microenvironment and stroma in treatment response and disease progression is widely recognized, routine histopathological assessments often overlook these components. Initial clinicians ' inspection of a sample of slides and heatmaps suggested that stroma adjacent to tumor cells frequently attracts signi fi cant attention cohort. In order to validate this this pattern we devised an unsupervised method for classifying the tissue types across all the WSIs in the PTRCHGSOC cohort (See the section Interpretability Analysis). This then allowedforinspectionofthedistributionofnearestneighbor ' s tissue types to the high attention patches containing epithelial tumor cells. We found that the top attention patches that were stroma tissue 25% were within 112 µm to tumor epithelial cells.

Furthermore, we found that in 56% of cases where the top attention patch is epithelial tumor tissue, stroma is found within 112 µm of this tissue (Supplementary Fig. 8c). This fi nding underscores the presence of valuable information within the non-tumor regions of pathological slides.

## Multimodal derived attention maps reveal interpretable spatial distributions from bulk proteomics

Extending this analysis into multimodal models, MCAT 21 and SurvPath 20 enable the visualization of more complex ' co-attention heatmaps. ' For each biological pathway, the model generates one ' gene-guided visual concept ' 21 for each patch in the WSI. These highlight important visual features integrated with proteomic data. The ability of the ' co-attention heatmaps ' (see Fig. 4d) to highlight spatial features can be compared to how deep neural networks can be trained to recognize objects in images that are accompanied bycaptions describing the image, even in the absence of detailed annotations describing where the speci fi c objects are within the entire image.

In our study, we apply a similar approach to understand histopathology whole slide images of tissues stained with H&amp;E, paired with corresponding proteomics data. Think of the histopathology images as the photographs and the proteomics data as the captions. The proteomics data describe the molecular ' story ' of the tissue, such as which proteins are present and in what abundance, indicating which molecular pathways are active. By training a multimodal AI network on these paired datasets, the network learns to associate speci fi c visual patterns in the histopathology images (like the arrangement and characteristics of cells and tissues) with the active molecular pathways described by the proteomics data.

Akeystrength of MCAT 21 and SurvPath 20 architectures is the ability to visualize these pathway-speci fi c attention maps over WSIs, thereby potentially discovering important morphological features related to treatment sensitivity or resistance. In Fig. 4 we showcase an example case study.

We found that the stroma adjacent to tumor cells frequently attracts signi fi cant attention in numerous slides and cases, underscoring the presence of valuable information within the non-tumor regions of pathological slides. Furthermore, we validate the robustness of these generated heatmaps with an ablation study (Supplementary Fig. 3a). See Supplementary Figs. 4, 5, and 6 for further case examples.

## Pathways and genes with high attention

The MCAT 21 and SurvPath 20 models are pioneering efforts in integrating multimodal data to enhance model interpretability within the domain of clinical outcomes. These models employ an early fusion technique that incorporates pathways; groups of proteins with interconnected functions, as fundamental components of their architecture. This approach leverages learnable embeddings to represent these pathways, generating a single value or ' attention ' that de fi nes how speci fi c pathways contribute to clinical endpoints. By incorporating pathway embeddings, these models facilitate a method of ranking the relative importance of different pathways in relation to a clinical outcome. To evaluate the pathway embeddings ' weights, we present the relative rank of each learned weight to highlight which pathways are most critical for the model ' s predictions (Supplementary Table 8).

## Discussion

A great number of biomarkers have been evaluated to predict platinum sensitivity in HGSOC 31 . Associative analysis of individual genes or coregulated gene groups did not identify accurate predictors of platinum response 31 . Evaluating the predictive power of the presence or absence of key biological functions, such as HR de fi ciency offered an alternative strategy. HRde fi ciency can be detected by various next-generation sequencing based methods 3 -5 but the predictive power of these methods was only validated for PARP inhibitor therapy 32 , and currently they are not used in the decision making for fi rst line platinum treatment. This is likely due to the fact, HR de fi ciency is predictive of platinum response only in certain biological context, such as the presence of alternative DNA repair pathways. Our hypothesis was that this biological context may be identi fi ed by extracting certain morphological features from WSI. Digital pathology and associated deep learning-based computer vision algorithms emerged as a possible methodtodetect suchcomplex biological functions such as HRD status and platinum sensitivity 8,9,16,33 .

Since molecular pro fi ling-based, biological pathway-based and digital pathology-basedpredictorsall showed a certain level of predictive power but none of those had suf fi ciently high accuracy alone, we hypothesized that these methods capture different aspects of platinum response/resistance and those could be combined to produce more accurate predictions. To test this hypothesis, we decided to integrate proteomics and histopathology. We foundthatourmodelscancapturenon-mutualinformationthatspansfrom molecular mechanisms to tissue-level manifestations of HGSOC. Proteomics provides the ' what ' in terms of molecular changes and potential targets, while histopathology provides the ' where ' and ' how ' these changes manifest in tissue architecture and the tumor microenvironment. Our results indicate that amalgamating information from these projections of the disease is bene fi cial for machine-learning models. This is concordant withotherrecentliterature where multimodal deep learning has been shown to improve model performance in risk strati fi cation of HGSOC using MRI, histopathology, and genomic signatures with a late fusion architecture 34,35 . Furthermore, including clinical variables has also been shown to enhance model performance 36 .

Our models are validated not only by consistent performance metrics across various train-test con fi gurations, but also by the rediscovery of pathways known to be of high importance for HGSOC platinum response, such as Homologous Recombination Repair 22 .

Proteomics is rarely used in the diagnostic setting, although reversephase protein arrays (RPPA) may provide a diagnostically applicable methodtoquantifyafewhundredproteins 37 . Combiningtranscriptomicsor epigenomic pro fi ling with WSI by the strategy outlined in this paper is a logical next step that may be easier to incorporate into the existing diagnostic pipelines. Additionally, joint embedding approaches, such as TANGLE 38 , present promising future directions. These methods enable models to be evaluated using only WSIs at test time while still leveraging proteomics knowledge during training for enhanced performance.

While our study demonstrates promising results, several limitations should be considered. These limitations may impact the generalizability of the proposed machine learning models in diverse clinical settings, as well as the robustness of the histopathological and proteomic biomarkers identi fi ed.

Machine learning algorithms generally rely on the assumption that data is independent and identically distributed (I.I.D), which is crucial for generalizing to unseen data 39 . However, real-world datasets often exhibit domain shifts, making them somewhat out of distribution (O.O.D) with respect to training cohorts. Speci fi cally, in this study, variations in H&amp;E staining routines, sample preservation, and imaging technologies across different institutions introduce such challenges.

We addressed these challenges through pathology-speci fi c self-supervised learning pre-training. While some argue that a properly pre-trained histopathology patch feature extractor mitigates such domain shift issues 40 , other domain-adaptation methods continue to enhance model generalization 41 . Ongoing work aims to overcome these issues with the development of open-source foundation models 23,42 and domain adaptation protocols.

Similarly, potential discrepancies in Liquid Chromatography coupled with Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) data due to institutional protocol differences were mitigated through comprehensive distribution overlap analyses, ensuring consistency in our biological signal capture. However, variations in signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) may still negatively affect model generalization.

It is notable that the combined WSI/proteomics predictor, in a linear combination with HRD measures, produced a predictor with an accuracy that may merit further consideration in a clinical setting, pending validation by independent cohorts. However, it is important to acknowledge that with 158 patients (348 samples) in the PTRC-HGSOC dataset and 127 patients (159 samples) in the TCGA dataset, the clinical cohorts used were relatively small. Patient numbers are limited due to the rarity of paired H&amp;E and proteomicsdata.Thesesmallsamplesizesmayimpactthegeneralizabilityof the results and model robustness. Furthermore, since none of these cohort are from clinical trials, the treatment protocols and outcome measures may not be as standardized as in a prospective clinical study. However, this also has some bene fi ts by capturing the heterogeneity of actual clinical practice. These factors underscore the need for validation in larger, more recent, and prospectively collected clinical cohorts as well as real-world datasets before considering these models for clinical application.

In conclusion we demonstrated that it is technically feasible to derive a complex predictor of platinum response by combining WSI image analysis and proteomic pro fi les obtained from debulking biopsies. Accurate prediction of treatment response may allow the early administration of other therapies, without waiting several months of failed platinum therapy. Such cases may bene fi t from novel therapy such as the recently approved antibody drug conjugate Mirvetuximab Soravtansine 43 .

## Methods

## Patient cohorts

This study aimed to predict response to platinum treatment in high-grade serous ovarian cancer by using supervised deep multi-modal learning with H&amp;EWSIsandproteomicLC-MS/MSdata.Basedonliteraturesearchthere were only 2 datasets with these data available: PTRC-HGSOC (158 patients, 348 samples with all 3 data types) 10 and TCGA (127 patients 159 samples with all 3 data types) 13 . Refractory cancers were de fi ned as those that progressed or had stable disease within 6 cycles of initial platinum/taxane therapy after initial debulking surgery 10 . Sensitive tumors were de fi ned as those that responded to initial platinum/taxane therapy and did not progress within 2 years. All samples were treatment-naive at the time of biopsy (See cohorts overview in Supplementary Table 7).

## Whole slide images

The PTRC-HGSOC dataset of 348 Formalin-Fixed, Paraf fi n-Embedded (FFPE) H&amp;E WSIs was downloaded from The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA) 44 https://www.cancerimagingarchive.net/collection/ptrc-hgsoc/ representing 158 unique patients. The bookend FFPE slides were cut at 4 µm thickness using a microtome and mounted on glass slides (Leica Biosystems Cat 3800040) for H&amp;E staining. Digital images of the H&amp;E slides were recorded using a ScanScope AT Slide Scanner (Leica Aperio Technologies, Vista, CA, USA) under ×20 objective magni fi cation (0.4965 µm per pixel resolution). The 159 relevant WSIs of the TCGA-OV dataset that were overlapping with the CPTAC-OV cohort were downloaded from The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA) 44 https://www.cancerimagingarchive.net/ collection/tcga-ov/ representing 127 unique patients TS1, TS2 (Fresh Frozen), DX1 and DX2 (FFPE) type slides. The TCGA-OV WSIs are heterogeneous in terms of scanner modalities, manufacturers, and acquisition protocols. In most cases, the images were acquired as part of routine care and not as part of a controlled research study or clinical trial. H&amp;E slides were readandembeddedunder20Xobjectivemagni fi cation(0.5040 µmperpixel resolution).

## Proteomic data

The TCGA Liquid Chromatography coupled with Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) proteomics data was downloaded from the Supplementary Information: Table 3 of Zhang et al. 13 . Following pre-processing and validation from the original manuscript, an average of 7952 proteins were measured per tumor. The PTRC-HGSOC proteomics data was downloaded from the deposited data table: processed LC-MRM-MS data 10 . Afterpre-processing 8,800 proteins were found in over 50% of the measured bio-specimens.

The overlapping set of proteins from the TCGA (CPTAC) and PTRCHGSOCdatasets were collected amounting to 7621 shared proteins. TCGA (CPTAC) and PTRC-HGSOC datasets were imputed with the scikit-learn KNNImputer 45 , log normalized, and standard scaled to remove any batch effect present in the data. The alignment of datasets was validated by testing the overlap of distributions of each protein. We employed the nonparametric statistical test the Kolmogorov- Smirnov (KS) test to compare the distribution of each protein between the two independent datasets. We found that they do not show a statistically signi fi cant difference: Mean KS Statistic: 0.112, Mean P -Value: 0.128.

## Feature extraction -histopathology

The WSIs were visualized to ensure quality, segmented to remove the slide background or scanner artifacts with the CLAM 22 WSI preprocessing pipeline, and patched into non-overlapping 224 × 224 pixel patches representing approximately 112 × 112 µm. To reduce the dimensionality of these large gigapixel images, patches were packed into tensor ' bags ' with dimensions n ; 3 ; 224 ; 224 ½  where n is the number of patches generated from a WSI and 3 represents the RGB channels.

To get a broad overview of model performance, these patches were then embedded by three pre-trained feature-extracting vision transformer foundation models to create three separate sets of embeddings covering all samples from all cohorts. These choices were made to understand the effects of pre-training scale vs diversity vs speci fi city on our task performance.

As a baseline, we tested one of the earlier histopathology-speci fi c foundation models made opensource; CTransPath 24,40 . Next, the UNI 23 model was chosen as it was pre-trained on non-TCGA data ensuring there was no possible data leak when testing downstream models on TCGA-OV WSI data. Furthermore, this model was pre-trained on more histopathology image data than CTransPath 24 (100 million vs 23 million H&amp;E tiles respectively).

Finally, to explore the utility of ' same domain ' pre-training, we pretrained our own vision transformer foundation model - DINO-OV on 16.2 million ovarian cancer speci fi c histopathology tiles gathered from 5 opensource H&amp;E WSI data (see Supplementary Table 5). This was done with the hypothesis that same domain pre-training may improve patch-feature representations for ovarian cancer speci fi c downstream tasks.

## Feature extraction -proteomics

Deliberately selecting and structuring proteomics data to add to multimodal deep learning models has an impact on fi nal model performance as well as the inherent interpretability of the model once trained. We investigated three high-level proteomics selection strategies; signature-based, subtype-based, and pathways-based.

Asignatures-based strategy involves selecting only one fi xed length 1 d vector also known as a ' proteomic signature ' which is a subset of the proteomics data. This vector is often found by machine learning based feature selection methods. In our study, we investigated the ' Chowdhury signature ' (see Platinum-response Protein Signature ( ' Chowdhury Signature ' )) as a proteomics feature.

Subtypes-based grouping offers another solution where nonoverlapping protein ' subtype ' groups form structures that form input features to the multimodal architectures. Finally, pathways represent unique cellular functions, they constitute appropriate basic reasoning units suitable for interpretability. For multi-modal models, MCAT 21 and SurvPath 20 , data was organized into such groups (see the sections CPTAC HGSOC subtypes ( ' CPTAC grouping ' ), Platinum Resistant Genes and Pathways ( ' PRG pathways ' ), Repair Mechanism Pathways ( ' RM pathways ' )). PorpoiseMMF models were trained with the same sets of proteins as these groups but instead as one concatenated vector of unique proteins as input.

These proteomics selection strategies resulted in two non-overlapping (see the sections see Platinum-response Protein Signature ( ' Chowdhury Signature ' ) and CPTAC HGSOC subtypes ( ' CPTAC grouping ' )) and two overlapping groupings (see the sections Platinum resistant genes and pathways ( ' PRG pathways ' ) and Repair mechanism pathways ( ' RM pathways ' )) to generate features as inputs to the models. The next sections describe these four signature, subtype, and pathways-based proteomic structured input features. All groupings and protein names used are released (See Data availability).

## Platinum-response protein signature ( ' Chowdhury Signature ' )

Chowdhury et al. 10 describe a 64-gene unweighted proteomic signature that predicts response to platinum treatment. This signature was identi fi ed by investigation of 22 pathways based on 30 years of literature 30 followed by nonlinear machine-learning-based feature selection. From these proteins, 60 were measured in both our training and validation sets. This signature was used as input to the PorpoiseMMF model. It was also possible to group these proteins into 5 non-overlapping groups: Drug Metabolism &amp; Biological Oxidation, Metabolic, Hypoxia, NK-kB as was described in Chowdhury et al. These sub-groups were used in the MCATandSurvPath architectures. This signature is used for the results shown in Table 1 and the section Predicting Survival -Prognostic Evaluation.

## CPTAC HGSOC subtypes ( ' CPTAC grouping ' )

Zhang et al. 13 describe 1664 proteins clustered into 7 proteomic subtypes from the non-overlapping WGCNA-derived modules pathways based on the enrichment of KEGG and Reactome ontologies. These groups are labeled: ' DNAreplication ' , ' cell-cell communications ' , ' cytokine signaling ' , ' erythrocyte and platelet ' , ' ECM interaction ' , ' complement cascade ' , and ' metabolism ' . This method aimed to inject subtype information into the model explicitly to see if it enhanced model accuracy.

## Platinum resistant genes and pathways ( ' PRG pathways ' )

Huang et al. 30 curated a list of over 900 proteins associated with platinum resistance in cancer, based on 30 years of literature. Of these, 587 were found across all patient samples in our studied cohorts. This list provides a comprehensive set of pathways and genes relevant for exploring HGSOC treatment responses, as it encompasses a wide range of biological processes involved in drug resistance. These processes include the regulation of drug dynamics, such as control over drug entry, exit, accumulation, sequestration, and detoxi fi cation within cells; DNA damage response, which refers to the enhanced ability to repair and tolerate platinum-induced DNA damage; cell survival pathways, where alterations enable cancer cells to avoid death despite chemotherapy; pleiotropic processes, which involve changes in multifunctional pathways affecting cancer cell behavior; and fi nally, variations in the tumor microenvironment, which may also contribute to drug resistance.

## Repair mechanism pathways ( ' RMpathways ' )

To investigate the role of DNA repair mechanisms-related pathways in predicting response to platinum-treatment we utilized the 36 DNA repairrelated pathways from MSigDB and KEGG. These pathways are more mechanistic than the ' PRG pathways ' section Platinum Resistant Genes and Pathways ( ' PRG pathways ' ) and are not constrained to known platinum response biological mechanisms.

## Model training

We trained all the deep learning based binary classi fi cation models for 15 epochs with gradient descent, the ADAM optimizer, and a learning rate of 5 × 10   4 . CLAM as well as multimodal models were trained using a binary cross-entropy loss. Models were checkpointed with early stopping during training and modelcheckpoints with the lowest validation loss were used for evaluation on test sets. We used L2 regularization with L = 1 × 10   5 and weighted sampling to make sure models were not biased by class imbalances as there were more sensitive/responder patients than refractory patients within and across cohorts. Full set of hyperparameters for experiments are found in Supplementary Table 6.

The full pipeline was trained on 3 Quadro RTX 8000 GPUS at the Budapest EMK server. Clinical metadata was checked for the PTRCHGSOC hospital hold-out experiments to see if platinum sensitivity could be predicted from this data. Random forest models were not able to predict the label with AUC = 0.47 for the UAB hold-out and AUC = 0.55 for the MC hold-out experiment. This ruled out the learning of co-variate signals that could bias results or allow for unwanted shortcut learning to take place.

## Interpretability analysis

Using trained CLAM models 22 , we visualized attention weights for each patchonheld-outtestsetWSIs.Patcheswithhigherattentionweightshavea greater in fl uence on the model ' s fi nal decision. By mapping these weights back to their respective patches, we generated heatmaps where brighter areas indicate higher attention, highlighting the regions most relevant to the model ' s prediction.

Toevaluate the distribution of high model attention across tissue types, we analyzed the HGSOC cohort using K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) on the highest attention (also referred to as top-1) attention-labeled embeddings generated from all WSIs from the CLAM model that was trained on the TCGA-OV cohort and tested on HGSOC. We selected k = 10 based on the assumption that this would provide a fair representation of tissue type clusters in the 1024-dimensional space 25 . The centroids of these clusters can be thought of as ' morphological prototypes ' 47 , representing distinct morphologies found in the HGSOC histopathology. Next, the 10 patches closest to each centroid were visualized and reviewed by a board-certi fi ed gynecological pathologist for tissue type classi fi cation. Finally, we counted the number of top-1 attention patches from each slide that corresponded to eachtissue class to give a fi nal distribution of tissue types that were attributed high attention (see Supplementary Fig. 7).

To evaluate tissue types that are found in close proximity to high attention patches we developed an unsupervised tissue labeling method similar to what was previously described - k-nearest neighbors (KNN) with 500 randomly sampled patch embeddings per WSI instead of only top-1 attention patches in the section Interpretability Analysis. We then analyzed the distribution of tissue classes among the nearest neighbors to highattention patches across the cohort. We used the k-tree method 48 to fi nd the nearest patches to the patch with the top attention and plotted the distribution of classes from the K-means over all patches in the dataset (see Supplementary Fig. 8).

To produce a ranking of pathway importance in the decision-making process for the model for this WSI Proteomics paired data we used the method of Integrated gradients (IGs) (See Methods Integrated Gradients). This was also performed for the proteins within each pathway to provide a ranking of protein importances to model decision making.
