## Abstract

Cancer prognosis prediction increasingly leverages multi-modal learning, yet existing approaches often rely on omics data that are costly and difficult to collect in routine practice. Pathology reports, by contrast, are routinely generated and widely available, providing a practical textual modality complementary to histology. We present

CALM ( C linical A nchor text guided L earning for M ulti-modal prognosis prediction), a general framework that integrates pathology images and reports through riskspecific anchor texts. CALM systematically incorporates prior clinical knowledge by guiding image and text alignment with large language model-derived anchors, further refined via few-shot tuning. Across 14 TCGA cancer types, CALM improved prognostic accuracy compared to image and text baselines (up to +11.5% mean C-index), with enhanced training stability. CALM achieved performance comparable to image-omics integration in PORPOISE (0.652 vs. 0.644), highlighting the prognostic value of text. External validation in an independent head and neck cancer cohort demonstrated that CALM improved generalization in zero-shot settings. Attentionbased interpretation further confirmed that CALM aligns diagnostic text with tumor regions. Together, CALM offers a simple, interpretable, and clinically viable strategy for prognosis prediction, expanding the role of routine pathology reports as scalable priors in multi-modal oncology AI.

Keywords Computational pathology, Multi-modality integration, Vision language modeling, Clinically aligned, Text anchoring

## Introduction

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of death worldwide and continues to place heavy demands on healthcare systems. Despite major research efforts, improving cancer  diagnosis  and  prognosis  is  still  a  major  challenge.  A  key  difficulty  lies  in  the  biological complexity and heterogeneity of cancer. These variations appear at many levels, from histopathological morphology to genomic and transcriptomic profiles, and extend across both tumor cells and their surrounding microenvironments [1]. Because of this complexity,  accurate  diagnosis  and  prognosis  require  more  than  pathological  imaging of whole slide images (WSIs). Instead, they depend on integrating multiple clinical

Recently, the rapidly evolving field of computational pathology (CPath) has emerged as a promising approach to address these challenges in cancer diagnosis and prognosis [2]. CPath leverages deep learning techniques applied to digitized WSIs to tackle a variety of histopathology-related tasks. The development of large-scale foundation models trained on extensive datasets has led to substantial performance improvements across diverse downstream applications [3-11]. These advancements have enabled significant progress in key clinical areas, including tumor grading [12], risk stratification [13, 14], subtyping [15-17], and prognosis prediction [18-25].

To overcome the performance limitations of WSI-only approaches, recent prognosis prediction studies have increasingly proposed various multi-modal integration methods. These include incorporating RNA sequencing, copy number variations, and mutations alongside imaging data [18], grouping hallmark genes into pathway-level representations for integration [19, 20], and combining with immunohistochemistry images [26]. While these omics-based multi-modal integration methods provide clear informational advantages, they face significant challenges in data acquisition complexity.

Obtaining  RNA  sequencing  data,  for  instance,  requires  separate  processing  protocols and substantial data generation costs. Although datasets like The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) provide well-curated paired data that serves as the foundation for most research studies, clinical settings present considerable collection difficulties, with paired data acquisition being exceptionally rare. Consequently, while omics integration offers demonstrable  benefits,  its  practical  applicability  in  real-world  clinical  environments remains limited.

To address these limitations, recent research has increasingly focused on image and text-based  multi-modal  methodologies.  Unlike  omics  data,  text  reports  are  routinely collected in clinical practice and have already been accumulated at scale in hospital databases, offering substantial untapped potential. Furthermore, while pathology reports are generated through image-based observation and interpretation, they represent information-rich modalities that encapsulate human expert experience and essential diagnostic insights critical for clinical decision-making.

Marini et al. [27] pioneered this approach for tissue type classification by parsing conclusion sections from pathology reports and aligning them with images using various alignment losses. Their image and text modality demonstrated superior predictive performance  compared  to  image-only  counterparts  while  enabling  cross-modal  retrieval capabilities. Similarly, TOP [28] introduced text-based prior knowledge to enable fewshot classification. Given that WSIs comprise complex assemblages of repetitive patch images typically requiring large-scale image datasets, TOP successfully boosted model learning performance by providing morphological characteristics descriptions for each tissue type.

MUSK [3] represents a pathology image and language foundation model built on the BEITv3 architecture. This patch-level image and caption alignment model has demonstrated excellent performance across multiple downstream tasks. To overcome limitations imposed by short text context lengths, MUSK employs structured prompts focused on  morphological  features  to  summarize  pathology  reports  for  model  input,  directly demonstrating that image and text modality can achieve improved prognosis prediction performance compared to image-only or text-only approaches.

To  accelerate  image  and  text  based  multi-modal  prognosis  prediction,  we  propose C linical A nchor text guided L earning for M ulti-modal prognosis prediction, abbreviated as CALM . Our approach makes several key contributions:

1.  CALM is a model-agnostic framework that can be applied to diverse image encoders and image and text encoder architectures, enhancing practical applicability.
2.  CALM can leverage a systematic anchor text strategy grounded in clinical domain knowledge to improve training stability (variance reduction with ratios as low as 0.84) and predictive performance (up to 10% mean C-index improvement).
3.  CALM  can  be  scaled  using  large  language  models  (LLMs)  to  generate  and  refine cancer type-specific anchor texts, enabling adaptation to diverse clinical contexts.

## Methods

## Overview of CALM

CALM extends patch-level vision-only or vision-language encoders to the slide level, enabling prognosis prediction based on slide-level image features and pathology reportderived  text  features.  In  addition,  risk-stage-specific  anchor  texts  are  introduced  to incorporate prior knowledge. A conceptual diagram of the CALM framework is shown in Fig. 1.

## Dataset and processing

CALM was trained and validated using two datasets: (1) 14 cancer types from the public TCGA cohort (details provided in Appendix A) and (2) an in-house head and neck cancer (HNSC) dataset from Kyung Hee University Medical Center (KHMC).

TCGA dataset For TCGA, we followed the setup of PORPOISE [18], using the same cancer types, patients, and publicly released split sets to ensure comparability. The data-

WholeSlideImage set  was  divided  into  80%  training  and  20%  testing  with  5-fold  cross-validation  at  the patient level, using one slide per patient. Overall survival (OS) times were discretized within each cancer type into four quantile-based bins (25% each) and encoded as labels from 0 to 3, with higher labels corresponding to lower relative risk. Unlike PORPOISE, which did not employ a validation set, CALM reserved 20% of the training set for validation to enable early stopping and model checkpoint selection. OS was used as the primary outcome variable.

KHMC-HNSC dataset The KHMC-HNSC dataset comprises a South Korean patient cohort and was used as an external validation set to assess model generalizability under domain shifts in ethnicity (predominantly East Asian), imaging devices, and acquisition protocols.  Of  160  enrolled  participants,  139  were  retained  after  excluding  cases  with missing data or incomplete labels (Institutional Review Board approval: Kyung Hee University Medical Center, KHNMC 2024-06-005-002).

Data preprocessing, splitting, and label generation followed the same pipeline as TCGA. The mean age of the cohort was 67.41 years, and 81.29% were male. The mean follow-up time was 30.30 months (±24.99). Among 139 patients, 15 deaths (10.79%) were observed. The mean follow-up was 15.15 months (±17.01) for censored patients and 32.13 months (±25.23) for deceased patients.

We employed KHMC-HNSC solely for zero-shot inference rather than training-validation. This design choice was motivated by (1) the limited sample size, (2) the small number of events leading to unstable model training, and (3) concerns regarding imbalance in follow-up durations between deceased and censored groups, which could bias model learning.

## WSI preprocessing

To  ensure  consistency  and  reproducibility,  we  employed  the  TRIDENT  pipeline  [29] for  WSI  preprocessing  and  feature  extraction.  TRIDENT  provides  standardized  functions for patch extraction and feature generation. Tissue segmentation was performed using GrandQC, and all patch analyses were conducted at 20× magnification (0.5 mpp). Default TRIDENT parameters were used unless otherwise specified. Patch size and preprocessing  settings  followed  encoder-specific  recommendations:  384 × 384  for  MUSK [3] and 256 × 256 for UNI v1 [11].

## Text report preprocessing

TCGA pathology  reports  are  originally  handwritten  and  digitized,  often  lengthy  and complex,  necessitating  preprocessing.  We  utilized  the  dataset  released  by  TCGAReports  [30],  which  applied  optical  character  recognition  (OCR)  to  convert  scanned reports into text. Since the extracted reports often exceeded model context length, we adopted  structured  summarization  following  MUSK  [3].  For  summarization,  we  used the  LLaMA 3.1 70B Instruct-tuned model [31]. Among the two text encoders in this study,  MUSK-L supports a maximum context length of 100 tokens, whereas BioMedBERT [32] supports 512 tokens. Accordingly, we designed two strategies: (1) short-context summarization and (2) long-context summarization. For cases in which pathology reports were unavailable, we inserted a placeholder sentence stating 'Text report is not available. '

## Short-context summarization

Since the MUSK-L encoder is limited to 100 tokens, MUSK adopted a structured prompt consisting of five morphology-related questions [3]. This approach generates a concise structural summary that can serve as model input. To summarize large-scale pathology reports, we followed structured prompts proposed by MUSK. The five categories are as follows:

- Tumor size :  What is the tumor size? Provide exact measurements if available and explain them.
- Differentiation : What is the histologic differentiation grade of the lesion?
- Lymph node metastasis : Is there evidence of lymph node involvement?
- Local invasion : Is there evidence of invasion into adjacent tissues or organs?
- Surgical margins : Are the surgical margins clear of tumor?

We  generated  few-shot  examples  based  on  MUSK's  structured  summaries  and  used them to prompt the LLM for report summarization. Example prompts are shown in Fig. B1.

## Long-context summarization

BioMedBERT allows for up to 512 tokens, enabling inclusion of more detailed information than the MUSK-based summaries. Summarization thus incorporated cancer type, primary site, tumor size, histologic differentiation, lymphovascular invasion (LVI), perineural  invasion  (PNI),  lymph  node  metastasis,  AJCC  stage,  biomarker  testing  results, mitotic activity, and other clinically relevant remarks. Few-shot examples were curated and used to construct prompts. Example prompts are shown in Fig. B2.

## Building anchor text

Anchor texts are descriptive sentences that capture histological features relevant to cancer-type-specific  risk  stratification.  They  serve  as  reference  signals  in  the  joint  image and  text  representation  learning  process,  guiding  the  model  toward  clinically  meaningful features. The primary goal of incorporating anchor texts is to sharpen decision boundaries and facilitate more interpretable and clinically aligned prognosis prediction.

While anchor texts could be manually curated by human experts, we employed LLMs to ensure scalability and pan-cancer applicability. Anchor texts were generated for three risk  strata  (low-,  intermediate-,  and  high-risk)  across  all  14  cancer  types,  with  both short- and long-context versions created. Risk strata were defined during label generation: patients with the shortest OS were categorized as high-risk, the two middle groups as intermediate-risk, and patients with the longest OS as low-risk. Anchor text generation and tuning were performed using a state-of-the-art LLM (GPT-5, OpenAI).

## Generalized anchor text

Using the structured summary categories as a basis, we prompted the LLM to generate plausible descriptions for each risk group. Short-context anchor texts were first created and refined, followed by long-context anchors derived from them. Example prompts are shown in Fig. B3 and generated anchor texts are available at Supplementary Tables S1 and S2.

## Few-shot anchor text tuning

The generalized anchor texts rely on the LLM's prior knowledge and may not fully capture  dataset-specific  characteristics.  To  refine  them,  we  performed  few-shot  anchor text tuning. Specifically, we sampled a small set of summarized pathology reports corresponding to each risk group and provided them as in-context examples, prompting the  LLM  to  adjust  the  generalized  anchor  texts  accordingly.  This  ensured  alignment with cohort-specific characteristics. To avoid data leakage, these samples were excluded from training and validation, and to reduce noise from censoring, only uncensored samples were used. Three reports from each risk group (low, intermediate, and high) were selected. Example prompts are shown in Fig. B4, and the refined anchor texts obtained via few-shot tuning are provided in Supplementary Tables S3 and S4.

## Experiments and evaluations

## Research questions

We conducted six analyses to address the following research questions:

## Q1. Is anchor text beneficial for prognosis prediction?

To investigate this, we compared the performance of CALM with that of a baseline image-and-text multi-modal prediction model. Specifically, we ablated the anchor component by setting the weight of L Anchor in  the  CALM loss to λ =  0,  and  compared the resulting performance with the full model.

## Q2. How can anchor text be effectively aligned to specific domains?

To answer this question, we compared the performance of generalized anchor textbased prognosis prediction with anchor text tuning through few-shot tuning. Few-shot anchor  tuning  leverages  actual  pathology  reports  to  domain-align  generalized  anchor text  with  the  cohort  distribution.  We  trained  and  evaluated  identical  CALM  models, varying only the anchor text used.

## Q3. Is the multi-modal model more beneficial than uni-modal models?

To assess the performance difference between uni-modal and multi-modal approaches, we compared image-only and text-only models with CALM. The uni-modal models  were  created  by  removing  the  cross-attention  module  and  training  only  the image encoder or text encoder. For the text encoder, BioMedBERT was fine-tuned using LoRA [35] ( r =  8, α =  16) to update only a subset of parameters. However, since MUSK is a BEITv3 [36]-based vision-language model, isolating the text encoder for training was not feasible; therefore, LoRA was not applied to MUSK. Logits were obtained by passing attention-pooled features (for images) or CLS-pooled features (for text) through classification heads.

## Q4. Is the image-and-text model comparable to image-and-omics models?

We compared CALM's performance with PORPOISE [18], a multi-modal model that integrates  images  with  multi-omics  data.  This  comparison  utilized  identical  patient cohorts and train-test splits, enabling indirect performance assessment. However, direct comparison  limitations  must  be  acknowledged:  PORPOISE,  developed  prior  to  the foundation model era, employs ResNet-based image feature extractors, whereas CALM leverages pathology-specific foundation models. Consequently, performance differences cannot be attributed solely to the comparative utility of text versus omics modalities. Nevertheless, this comparison provides valuable insights into the potential information content of text-based features relative to established multi-modal approaches.

## Q5. Does CALM generalize to in-house datasets?

To  assess  the  generalizability  of  CALM  to  in-house  data,  we  evaluated  it  on  the KHMC-HNSC cohort.  We  compared  MUSK-based  CALM  against  several  baselines, including image-only, text-only, and image and text models. Given the limited sample size  and  severe  class  imbalance,  we  performed  zero-shot  inference-based  evaluation rather  than  full  fine-tuning  on  the  KHMC-HNSC  dataset.  Specifically,  we  employed five models pretrained on TCGA-HNSC and tested them directly on the entire KHMCHNSC cohort.

## Q6. Can CALM perform cross-modality queries?

Since CALM learns alignment between patch-level images and text tokens, it naturally  supports  cross-modality  queries  and  retrieval  tasks.  To  evaluate  this  capability and enhance interpretability, we used structured pathology summaries as text queries, retrieved  the  topK most  similar  image  patches,  and  assessed  their  clinical  relevance based on pathologist-provided tumor annotations. In addition, we visualized attention heatmaps across entire WSIs to identify which patch instances the model focused on.

## Evaluation metrics

Each CALM model was trained and evaluated five times per cancer type. We primarily report the average concordance index (C-index) to evaluate prognosis prediction performance. The C-index measures whether relative risk rankings are correctly ordered: 0.5 indicates chance level, while 1.0 corresponds to perfect prediction. In addition, we report the variance of the C-index and introduce the variance ratio metric, defined as the ratio of the standard deviation of the C-index for the target model over that of the baseline model. A variance ratio below 1 indicates greater stability (lower variance) of the target model. This metric was introduced to account for the inherent instability of prognosis prediction, where results may fluctuate across runs.

## Implementation details

CALM was implemented in Python (v3.10) using PyTorch (v2.4.0). All experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA H200 GPU (141 GB). The batch size was set to 1 with a gradient accumulation factor of 32. During training, we randomly sampled 4096 patchlevel  instances  per  WSI  to  construct  feature  bags,  following  the  strategy  proposed  in [19]. This approach balances computational efficiency and generalization. For validation and testing, all available patch instances from each slide were used to ensure comprehensive evaluation.

All models were trained with 5-fold cross-validation for up to 100 epochs, with early stopping based on validation loss. The best-performing model checkpoint was selected according to the lowest validation loss. We used the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10 -5 . Automatic mixed precision (AMP) was employed throughout training to accelerate computation and reduce memory usage.

## Results

To address our research questions, we evaluated the effects of CALM. The corresponding results are summarized in Tables 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5, with additional details provided in Supplementary Tables S5 and S6.

## Effects of anchor text

The employment of anchor text yielded two primary benefits: (1) enhanced training stability and (2) improved predictive performance (Table 1). Compared to image and text modeling approaches, CALM with anchor text demonstrated a mean C-index improvement of 2.43% (with improvements observed in 10 out of 14 cancer types) when using UNI and BioMedBERT, accompanied by a variance ratio of 0.84, indicating substantially improved stability.

The  performance  gains  were  particularly  pronounced  when  using  MUSK-V  and MUSK-L models. MUSK-based CALM achieved a substantial mean C-index improvement of 11.45% (13 out of 14 cancer types), with a variance ratio of 1.01, representing a  marginal  increase  in  variance.  Overall,  the  pathology  vision-language  model  MUSK demonstrated superior performance compared to UNI and BioMedBERT. This advantage can be attributed to MUSKs pre-training process, which effectively learns semantic alignment between vision and language modalities, enabling it to leverage semantic meaning-based anchoring more effectively than UNI and BioMedBERT.

## Effects of few-shot anchor text tuning

Proper curation of anchor text is essential, as it serves dual functions: regularizing integrated image and text features while facilitating prior knowledge injection. To examine the impact of anchor text quality on model performance, we compared two strategies: (1)  LLM-generated generalized risk  descriptions  and  (2)  few-shot  anchor  text  tuning. The  generalized  risk  descriptions  leverage  the  comprehensive  knowledge  of  LLMs  to provide broadly applicable descriptions. In contrast, few-shot anchor text tuning examines  actual  reports  for  each  risk  level  and  refines  the  generally  generated  knowledge accordingly. These results are presented in Table 2.

The general anchor text generated using LLMs demonstrated higher C-indices compared to image and text models alone (UNI and BioMedBERT: 0 . 614 → 0 . 620 ;  MUSKV and L: 0 . 590 → 0 . 618 ).  However, the final models with few-shot anchor text tuning achieved mean C-indices of 0.626 and 0.652 for UNI and BioMedBERT, and MUSK-V and L, respectively, representing performance improvements of 1.4% and 5.7% after tuning.  Anchor  text  refining  showed  greater  improvement  in  MUSK-based  CALM,  confirming that better alignment between image and text modalities yields larger benefits.

Notably, for KIRC  and  KIRP  cancer  types, CALM  with  general  anchor  text actually showed lower performance than without anchoring (MUSK; KIRC: 0 . 649 → 0 . 603 , KIRP: 0 . 669 → 0 . 652 ). However, after few-shot anchor text tuning, these models showed substantial improvements of 12.32% and 15.92%, respectively, achieving better predictive performance than the image and text baseline (KIRC: 0 . 649 → 0 . 678 , KIRP: 0 . 669 → 0 . 756 ).

Anchor text tuning had minimal impact on variance ratio improvements. The variance ratios for UNI and BioMedBERT remained at 1.00, while MUSK-V and L showed 1.06.

## Comparison with uni- and multi-modal integration

To evaluate the effect of uni-modal versus multi-modal integration, we compared prognosis prediction models trained with image-only, text-only, and CALM (image and text). The results are summarized in Table 3.

## UNI and BioMedBERT group

Within the UNI and BioMedBERT group, CALM outperformed the uni-modality models  in  8  of  14  cancer  types,  showing  an  average  improvement  of  +1.62%  compared  to image-only and +13.61% compared to text-only. The image-only setting with UNI features achieved the highest performance in 4 cancer types (COADREAD, HNSC, KIRP, LIHC), whereas the text-only setting was superior only in SKCM. These findings suggest two potential explanations: (1) BioMedBERT may be limited by domain mismatch with pathology corpora, and (2) alignment between UNI and BioMedBERT was not sufficiently established.

Domain limitations  of  BioMedBERT Although  BioMedBERT  achieved  competitive results  in  some  cases  (e.g.,  KIRP:  0.709,  SKCM:  0.613),  its  performance  was  generally modest, often near or below 0.5. This likely reflects the fact that BioMedBERT was pretrained on PubMed corpora, which differ from the terminology and structure of pathology reports. Thus, while BioMedBERT can leverage longer contexts, it may fail to capture the semantic nuances and inter-term relationships specific to pathology text.

Insufficient  alignment  between  UNI  and  BioMedBERT Beyond the  corpus  limitations of BioMedBERT, the anchor text used for alignment may not have been sufficient to enforce strong cross-modal correspondence between heterogeneous encoders. Although CALM improved performance in 57% of cancer types, the observation that image-only settings still yielded the highest results in some cancers suggests that cross-modal integration was not always effective. Prior work (Marini et al. [27]) has highlighted the importance of  combining multiple loss terms to explicitly match heterogeneous embedding spaces. Similarly, when applying CALM with diverse encoders, stronger alignment strategies beyond weak anchor-based alignment may be required.

## MUSK-V and L group

In contrast, MUSK-V and L were pretrained on paired pathology images and captions, resulting in well-aligned image and text representations. Consequently, the performance gap between image-only and text-only models was relatively small (image-only mean: 0.609;  text-only  mean:  0.617).  Notably,  text-only  performance  (mean  0.617)  exceeded that of BioMedBERT (0.551), reflecting the benefit of domain-specific corpora exposure. In this group, CALM yielded a greater overall benefit than in the UNI and BioMedBERT setting  (macro  average:  0.626 → 0.652;  +7.06%  vs  image-only,  +5.67%  vs  text-only).

CALM achieved the best results in 12 of 14 cancer types, with image-only being highest in LIHC and text-only in LUSC.

## Notable patterns in specific cancer types

For  PAAD,  both  image-only  and  text-only  models  showed  limited  predictive  ability (0.494-0.547). However, CALM improved performance to 0.584-0.593, indicating that image and text integration can provide complementary value beyond individual modalities.  Conversely,  in  LIHC,  image-only  achieved  the  highest  performance,  while  textonly lagged by 13.84-18.67%. As a result, multi-modal integration did not surpass the stronger single modality. This case highlights that when a substantial imbalance exists between modalities, integration gains may be diminished or even counterproductive.

## Comparison with multi-modal omics integration

To assess the practical utility of CALM, we compared its prognostic performance with PORPOISE  [18],  a  representative  multi-modal  framework  that  integrates  pathology images with bulk RNA sequencing, copy number variation, and mutation profiles (Table 4). Both models were trained and validated on identical datasets and patient splits.

In PORPOISE, the omics-only model achieved a mean C-index of 0.606, while multimodal  fusion  with  images  increased  performance  to  0.644.  CALM  with  UNI  and BioMedBERT  encoders  attained  a  mean  C-index  of  0.626,  exceeding  the  omics-only baseline  but  remaining  below  PORPOISE's  multi-modal  fusion.  MUSK-based  CALM reached a mean C-index of 0.652, which was close to the performance of PORPOISE's multi-modal  model.  Although  the  comparison  is  not  strictly  one-to-one,  since  PORPOISE relied on ResNet50 features whereas CALM benefits from modern foundation encoders,  this  trend  suggests  that  text-based  clinical  context  may  provide  prognostic information that partially overlaps with, or complements, molecular data.

At  the  cancer-type  level,  PORPOISE  showed  pronounced  advantages  in  KIRP  and PAAD, achieving C-indices of 0.816 and 0.653, respectively, compared with 0.756 and 0.584 from MUSK-based CALM. These findings are consistent with PORPOISE's report that certain cancers derive greater benefit from molecular features. Conversely, CALM outperformed PORPOISE in LIHC (0.700 vs. 0.622) and UCEC (0.713 vs. 0.634). Taken together, these patterns highlight that modality contributions are cancer-specific rather than  uniform,  and  reinforce  the  complementary  nature  of  clinical  text  and  molecular data for prognostic modeling.

## External validation

To assess the generalization and robustness of CALM, we evaluated its zero-shot prognosis prediction performance on an external dataset, the KHMC-HNSC cohort (Table 5).

Image-only  (0.563)  and  image  and  text  baseline  models  (0.577)  demonstrated  comparable  or  slightly  higher  predictive  performance  than  CALM  with  general  anchors (0.562).  In  contrast,  the  text-only  baseline  (0.491)  performed  markedly  worse,  potentially reflecting domain shifts in pathology reports across populations and institutions. This suggests that directly applying text encoders without adaptation may fail to capture cohort-specific reporting characteristics.

In comparison, CALM with refined anchors achieved a C-index of 0.592, corresponding to improvements of 5.34% over general-anchor CALM and 2.60% over the image and text  baseline.  These  results  indicate  that  refined  anchors  may  better  capture  domainrelevant textual cues and provide a form of regularization that stabilizes model predictions compared to text-only training. Given that the refined anchors were derived from TCGA-HNSC, this finding suggests their potential to transfer high-level diagnostic patterns  even  across  heterogeneous  domains.  While  the  margin  over  the  image  and  text baseline  remains  modest  in  the  zero-shot  setting,  additional  fine-tuning  or  few-shot anchor tuning on KHMC-HNSC could plausibly yield greater gains.

## Discussion

In this study, we introduced CALM, a strategy that leverages anchor texts to improve image and text based prognosis prediction in cancer. Unlike omics data, which require specialized assays with substantial costs and time, pathology reports are routinely generated and widely available in clinical practice. This makes text-guided multimodal learning a more practical and clinically translatable approach.

Our results demonstrate that prior knowledge integration, originally applied in classification  tasks,  can  be  effectively  extended  to  prognosis  prediction.  Importantly,  we showed  that  refining  anchors  with  few-shot  tuning  leads  to  substantial  performance gains  compared  to  using  only  general  LLM-derived  anchors.  CALM  improved  across both  uni-modal  pathology  encoders  and  multimodal  vision-language  settings,  suggesting  its  broad  applicability.  Moreover,  indirect  comparison  with  omics-augmented models (e.g., PORPOISE) indicates that text can provide information complementary to histology at a level approaching omics.

External  validation  under  zero-shot  conditions  further  highlighted  CALM's  robustness. Even with limited and imbalanced data, refined anchors improved generalization beyond baseline  image  and  text  models.  Together,  these  findings  suggest  that  CALM provides a simple, interpretable, and clinically viable alternative for integrating textual priors into pathology-based prognosis prediction.

Nonetheless,  several  limitations  remain.  First,  gains  were  limited  when  extending vision-only encoders such as UNI, suggesting that anchor texts alone may provide insufficient  supervision  for  aligning  heterogeneous  feature  spaces.  Second,  PubMedBERT underperformed,  likely  due  to  domain  mismatch  between  biomedical  literature  and pathology reports; pathology-specific language models or continual training may yield greater benefit. Finally, MUSK was constrained by a short context length (100 tokens), which restricted the incorporation of full report details beyond morphological descriptions. In addition, while we employed structured summarization of pathology reports, future  work  should  compare  this  approach  with  explicitly  extracted  clinical  variables such as TNM staging to assess the relative contributions of structured features versus free-text  representations.  Future  studies  should  therefore  explore  stronger  alignment strategies, pathology-specific language models, long-context vision-language architectures, and structured clinical features to fully capture the richness of pathology reports.
