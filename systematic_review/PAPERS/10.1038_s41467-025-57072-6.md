## Systematic inference of super-resolution cell spatial pro fi les from histology images

Received: 14 September 2024

Accepted: 7 February 2025

Check for updates

Peng Zhang 1,7 , Chaofei Gao 1,7 , Zhuoyu Zhang 1 , Zhiyuan Yuan 2 , Qian Zhang 1 , Ping Zhang 3 , Shiyu Du 4 , Weixun Zhou 5 , Yan Li 6 &amp; Shao Li 1

Inferring cell spatial pro fi les from histology images is critical for cancer diagnosis and treatment in clinical settings. In this study, we report a weaklysupervised deep-learning method, HistoCell, to directly infer super-resolution cell spatial pro fi les consisting of cell types, cell states and their spatial network from histology images at the single-nucleus-level. Benchmark analysis demonstrates that HistoCell robustly achieves state-of-the-art performance in terms of cell type/states prediction solely from histology images across multiple cancer tissues. HistoCell can signi fi cantly enhance the deconvolution accuracy for the spatial transcriptomics data and enable accurate annotation of subtle cancer tissue architectures. Moreover, HistoCell is applied to de novo discovery of clinically relevant spatial organization indicators, including prognosis and drug response biomarkers, across diverse cancer types. HistoCell also enable image-based screening of cell populations that drives phenotype of interest, and is applied to discover the cell population and corresponding spatial organization indicators associated with gastric malignant transformation risk. Overall, HistoCell emerges as a powerful and versatile tool for cancer studies in histology image-only cohorts.

Histology images are widely regarded as the cornerstone for disease diagnosis and treatment, and they provide in-depth insights for the examination of tissue architecture at a high-resolution level 1,2 . Specifi cally, as the fundamental components of tissue architecture, cells and their spatial organization within histology images contain suf fi cient information for the prediction of various clinical outcomes 3 . For example, identifying cancer cell nuclei and their morphological features at different locations within routinely available whole slide images (WSIs) is an important step in cancer grading 4 and diagnosis 5,6 . In parallel, determining lymphocytes nuclei from cancer tissue enables downstream analysis of tumor-in fi ltrating lymphocytes (TILs) 7 , which have been implicated in cancer recurrence 8 . Therefore, inferring super-resolution cell spatial pro fi les from histology images is prerequisite and fundamental for understanding disease progression, treatment responses, and mechanism by which the cell spatial organization performs biological functions.

Recent advancements in arti fi cial intelligence (AI) algorithms in digital pathology have enabled the automatic identi fi cation and classi fi cation of a large number of cells within WSIs 9 -12 , making it possible to infer cell spatial pro fi les directly based on histology morphological features 13 . Previously, the common paradigm for predicting the cell types of individual nuclei in histology images was fully-supervised with annotations by expert pathologists 10,11 . Although these fully-supervised models have indicated the associations between histological morphologies and cellular pro fi les, they are still limited in clinical practice due to the need of substantial manual time and the restriction of analysis to speci fi c nuclear types 14 . Besides, the performance of these models relies heavily on expert priors, which can be in fl uenced by low interrater agreement across pathology experts 15 and high variance of nuclear appearance in each WSI 16 . Alternatively, as the accumulation of bulk molecular omics data with paired histology images, certain AI models have been used to predict the genetic properties of tumors directly from WSIs 4,15,17 -21 , where labels for supervised prediction tasks are acquired by sequencing methods. In this regard, weaklysupervised paradigms, such as multi-instance learning 17,18 (MIL), have been adopted since the label is only de fi ned for the WSI instead of every single tile. During the weak supervision process, each tile ' s contribution to the slide-level label is quanti fi ed and aggregated for slide-level prediction. Accordingly, a series of weakly-supervised AI models have been proposed to predict expression pro fi les 4,15,17,19,22 , genetic alterations 18,23 and cellular pro fi les 24 -26 from histology image alone without manual annotation. In conclusion, despite limited by low-resolution, these studies indicated the ability of weakly-supervised AI algorithms to learn spatial patterns and augment existing data information. It might offer new perspectives to address the cellular spatial heterogeneity in digital pathology.

The rapid advancement of spatial transcriptomic (ST) technologies, especially sequencing-based ones, has made it possible to measure gene expression within the original tissue context 27,28 , enabling us to characterize spatial gene expression patterns and cellular pro fi les at an unprecedented resolution. Empowered by these technologies, several machine learning methods have been developed to predict spatial gene expression pro fi le from histology images with pre-training process on ST data, showing much higher resolution than those pretrained on bulk expression data 22,29 . For instance, a deep-learning model, named iStar 30 , was recently developed to predict the nearsingle-cell-level gene expression leveraging the histology image obtained from ST data, which reinforced the close associations between molecular traits and morphological features. Motivated by the superior performance of spatial expression pro fi le prediction, some algorithms have also been proposed to predict cell spatial profi les by integrating spatial transcriptomics and histology images based on spot-level deconvolution 24 . For example, an elegant ST analysis method, named POLARIS, has been recently proposed to predict spotlevel cell composition solely from histology images. It is demonstrated that histological images could conceptually provide important and complementary information including anatomical structure and distribution of cells 24 . Despite substantial advances, existing methods usually suffered from obvious limitations in terms of the inference of super-resolution cell spatial pro fi les from histology images. From the perspective of spatial resolution, these methods currently only infer the cell composition at the spot-level rather than single-nucleus-level, as they underuse the information provided by individual cell instance. Fromtheperspective of cellular resolution, these methods usually lack the in-depth discovery of hierarchical cellular pro fi les, including diverse cell states of each cell type, impeding the ability to dissect the cellular heterogeneity of histology images. Moreover, it remains elusive in terms of the clinical value of the extracted morphological features and their spatial features from histology images across diverse cancer types 9,14 . Consequently, there is still a signi fi cant methodological challenge for accurate inference of super-resolution cellular pro fi les from histology images and exploration of their clinical values.

In this work, we present HistoCell, a weakly-supervised deep learning method for accurate super-resolution cell spatial pro fi le inference at the single-nucleus-level. HistoCell is based on the systematic association decoupling between histological morphological features from images and hierarchical cellular information from ST expression data during training process. We evaluate the accuracy and robustness of HistoCell on various publicly available datasets from multiple cancer tissues. We also assess the ability of HistoCell in enhancing the cell-type deconvolution for the spot-based ST data by leveraging the predicted single-nucleus-level cell spatial pro fi les. Moreover, we explore the generalizability and versatility of HistoCell across diverse clinical contexts, including tissue architecture annotation, clinically relevant spatial organization indicators discovery and image-based cellular marker identi fi cation. Our research provides a versatile analysis tool for histology images from the spatial cellular perspective and bridges the gap between cell spatial organization and cancer clinical outcomes.

## Results

## The methodological framework of HistoCell

In order to address the challenge in predicting histology imagerelated cell spatial pro fi les, we proposed a weakly-supervised and annotation-free histology image analysis method, named HistoCell, to infer super-resolution cell spatial pro fi les consisting of cell types, cell states and their spatial network at the level of individual nuclei for histology images (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1). Methodologically, the cornerstone of HistoCell lies in the development of the pre-trained models that decoupled the associations between image features of segmented nuclei instances, and the deconvoluted cellular compositions within individual spots in ST data. Here, histology image features were derived through extracting morphological and topological features of individual nuclei, followed by hierarchically encoding them from the perspective of cell compartments, cell types and cell states. This allowed us to incorporate the prior knowledge related to cellular hierarchical classi fi cation, including the cell types to which cell states belongs and the tissue regions or cell compartments to which cell types belongs, so as to re fl ect the global information and fully extract histology image features. Then, the pre-trained models were obtained by performing weakly supervised learning with predicting the deconvoluted spot-level cellular composition based on the encoded singlenucleus-level histology image features in ST data (Methods).

In this study, the HistoCell pre-trained models were separately established for nine cancer types, on the basis of publicly available high-quality ST data from a total of 99 cancer samples (Supplementary Table 1, Methods), including breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA, n =13 patients/41 samples/33,059 spots), kidney renal cell carcinoma (KIRC, n =24 samples/20,776 spots), prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD, n =5 patients/10 samples/33,239 spots), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC, n =7 patients/10 samples /42,164 spots), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD, n =2 patients/9 samples/ 2970 spots), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD, n =2 patients/2 samples/4219 spots), ovarian cancer (OV, n =1 patient/1 sample/3455 spots), colon adenocarcinoma(COAD, n =1 patient/1 sample/2680 spots), and lung squamous carcinoma (LUSC, n =1 patients/1 sample/3858 spots). For each ST data, both expression data and co-registered high-resolution histology images were collected. It was worth noting that the pre-trained models could be still established in the case of few samples for certain cancer types since the model training was conducted across ST spots. Then, weconducteddeconvolution analysis on the spatial expression data to acquire spot-level cellular compositions as the weak supervision labels (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 1). Speci fi cally, the proportions of cell types within individual spots were obtained by four state-of-the-art deconvolution methods, including CARD 31 , RCTD 32 , Tangram 33 and POLARIS 24 , with the matched scRNA-seq dataset as reference (Supplementary Table 2). Furthermore, the cellular compartments, including epithelial ( ' Epi ' ), microenvironment ( ' TME ' ) and mixture ones ( ' Mix ' ) were fi rst annotated in terms of the relative proportion of deconvoluted epithelial and TME cell types within individual spots. Following that, the compositions of well-recognized epithelial cell states were inferred with EcoTyper 34 , resulting in the hierarchical cellular information of each ST spot (Methods).

To assess the rationality and robustness of the methodological framework of HistoCell, we fi rstly conducted cross-validation analysis on the ST data for nine cancer types individually (Supplementary Table 3). For each round, the HistoCell model was trained on the training spots and then used to predict the single-nucleus-level cell types for the validation spots solely based on histology images. Then, the predicted single-nucleus-level cells in each spot were clumped to mimic spot-level cell proportions, and the Pearson correlation coef fi cients (PCC) between the predicted and the originally deconvoluted cell proportions was adopted as one of the benchmark metrics. As a result, for all involved nine cancer types, we consistently observed that the spot-level proportions of known cell types predicted by HistoCell were signi fi cantly correlated with the original deconvoluted proportions across intra-sample and inter-sample slides ( p &lt;0.001, Supplementary Fig. 2). For each round of cross-validation, we furtherly compared the performance of HistoCell against the state-of-the-art algorithm POLARIS 24 , which predicts spot-level cell proportions by integrating histology images. We found that HistoCell signi fi cantly outperformed POLARIS in predicting spot-level cell proportions for all cell types solely from histology images across breast, prostate and liver cancer samples, where the average PCC of all cell types over these three cancer was enhanced 3.31 times ( p &lt;0.01, Fig. 2a -c). Speci fi cally, for low-abundance cell types within the ST data that could not be well predicted by POLARIS, including T cells, myeloid cells and stromal cells, HistoCell still generated signi fi cantly correlated cell proportions consistent with the original deconvolution ones, demonstrating its superior performance. Additionally, the rationalities of loss functions and training strategies used in HistoCell have been validated (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Finally, the fi nal pre-trained models were separately established for nine cancer types, with combining all ST samples for each cancer type as training data (Supplementary Table 3, Methods). Consistently, it was also observed that the separate pre-trained models outperformed the single model with training on all cancer samples combined, in predicting single-nucleus-level cell types across all nine cancer types. These fi nal pre-trained models were furtherly used for validation analysis on external datasets (Supplementary Table 4) and downstream application experiments (Supplementary Table 5).

## HistoCell infers super-resolution cell information solely from histology images

We assessed the ability of HistoCell to predict cell types at the singlenucleus-level based on the histology images in the external datasets with the manual annotations of single nucleus (Supplementary Table 4). Speci fi cally, PanNuke 41 dataset was applied to evaluate the cell type prediction performance of HistoCell. PanNuke dataset is a publicly available histology image dataset with single-cell type manual annotations (tumor, immune and stromal cells) of 196,468 nuclei across 19 cancer types in total. To numerically evaluate the performance of our model, one weakly-supervised model, Multiple Instance Learning (MIL)-based model, and two fully-supervised models, HoVerNet [10] and MaskRCNN [44] , were introduced as comparison. Here, the MIL-based model was developed with training on the cell compositions within multiple instances same as those in HistoCell, while HoVerNet and MaskRCNN were developed with training directly on annotated histology images from PanNuke (Methods).

As a result, the cell type predictions obtained by HistoCell were visually more consistent with the manual annotations, especially for tiles containing a mixture of different cell types (Fig. 2d). ROC curves indicated that the area under the curve (AUC) values of tumor, immune and stromal cells predicted by HistoCell on breast cancer histology images reached 0.93, 0.89 and 0.81, respectively, all of which were signi fi cantly greater than those predicted by the MIL-based weak supervision model ( p &lt;0.05, Fig. 2e). Surprisingly, HistoCell signi fi cantly outperformed even two fully supervised models, HoVerNet and MaskRCNN, in predicting tumor and immune cells. In the prediction of stromal cells, HistoCell still performed signi fi cantly better than MaskRCNN, although it was lower than HoVerNet. We speculated that it might be due to the low number of stromal cells across the histology images in the training data. Similarly, we found that HistoCell ' s predictive advantage was also present in other cancer types in the PanNuke dataset (Supplementary Fig. 4a -f).

Additional benchmark analysis was conducted on 10× Xenium breast cancer sample 42 with in-situ sequencing as annotations (Fig. 2f, Methods). We found that HistoCell exhibited superior prediction performance over HoVerNet on cancer epithelial cells, T cells and stromal cells, all of which HoVerNet was able to predict ( p &lt;0.01, Fig. 2g). Expectedly, it was also found that HistoCell performed better on the predictions of these three cell types than other comparison models, including the MIL-based model and MaskRCNN. More importantly, HistoCell was able to predict histology image-related cell pro fi les at a fi ner granularity than HoVerNet. For some cell types that HoVerNet could not predict, such as B cells and myeloid cells in immune cells, and endothelial cells in stromal cells, HistoCell still showed considerable prediction performance (AUC for B cells: 0.75; myeloid cells: 0.71; endothelial cells: 0.72). The above observations suggest that our model achieves the prediction performance comparable to that of the fully supervised model, and also enabled the cellular prediction with fi ner granularity.

Given the high heterogeneity in the epithelial cell population 34 , we further evaluated the ability of HistoCell to predict the cell states of individual tumor cells de fi ned by Ecotyper 34 (Methods). By predicting the cell state for each tumor cell in the sample derived from 10× Genomics with the sample id of ' Visium\_FFPE\_Human\_Breast\_Cancer ' (10x\_BRCA), we found that the proportions of predicted cell states across cancer epithelial spots were highly correlated with the original proportions inferred by Ecotyper across different spots through both intra-sample and inter-sample validation (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). Moreover, we found that HistoCell could clearly identify different cell states within epithelial cells and reveal their spatial distribution (Fig. 2h, i, Supplementary Fig. 5c -e). As expected, cells marked as the angiogenesis-related cell state ( ' Pro-angiogenic ' ) were spatially located at the interface between epithelial and stromal cells, whereas cells marked as the in fl ammation-related state ( ' Pro-in fl ammatory ' ) were spatially closer to immune cells. Thus, our method holds signi fi cant importance for accurate identi fi cation of histological morphological features with clear biological signi fi cance and interpretability.

## HistoCell enhances cellular deconvolution of spatial transcriptomic data

Cell type deconvolution at each spot is a critical issue recognized in spot-based ST data analysis. It has been demonstrated that histological images provide important and complementary information for spatial expression analysis of ST data 20,21,45 . This prompted us to apply HistoCell to enhance cell-type deconvolution for independent ST data by incorporating the predicted single-nucleus-level cellular pro fi les solely from co-registered histology images.

To validate it, a regression-based integration model (denoted as ' HistoCell-integration ' ) was leveraged to integrate spot-level cellular compositions deconvoluted from expression data and those inferred by HistoCell from co-registered histology images (Methods). Speci fi cally, we applied the HistoCell-integration model to the 10× Xenium breast cancer samples consisting of both co-registered high-resolution histology images and single-cell level expression data to generate the ground truth cell proportions across simulated ST spots (Fig. 3a). As a result, spot-level cell proportions inferred by HistoCell-integration more closely resembled the ground truth ones than those deconvoluted from expression data only (Fig. 3b). By calculating the root mean square error (RMSE) between the inferred and ground truth cell compositions, it was indicated that the cell proportions inferred by HistoCell-integration showed signi fi cantly lower RMSE than the deconvolution proportions ( p &lt;0.01), validating the important role of integrating the cellular pro fi les derived from histology images in ST data deconvolution analysis. Furthermore, we systematically assessed the deconvolution performance of the HistoCell-integration with comparison to 8 state-of-the-art deconvolution algorithms that were selected in terms of their superior performances in previous benchmark studies 46 . It was found that the cell proportions obtained by HistoCell-integration showed the lowest RMSE values among these algorithms (Fig. 3c). Moreover, we also observed that the HistoCellintegration model outperformed these 8 comparison deconvolution algorithms in terms of a series of evaluation metrics, including the jaccard similarity distance (JSD), Pearson correlation coef fi cient (PCC) and structural similarity index (SSIM) (Fig. 3d -f), demonstrating the superior performance of HistoCell-integration in cell type deconvolution. In summary, HistoCell offers an effective tool for conducting high-precision deconvolution analysis for ST data by integrating histological morphological features and spatial expression data.

## HistoCell enables the identi fi cation of clinically relevant spatial organization indicators across cancer types

Recent cancer digital pathology studies have highlighted the importance of cell spatial organization in understanding cancer biology and predicting clinical outcomes 3 . Given the superior performance of HistoCell in cell spatial pro fi le inference from histology images, we speculated that it could help us examine the spatial organization of multiple cell types within tumor tissues and its underlying mechanism.

First, focusing on cancer cells, we applied HistoCell to systematically dissect cell morphological features across diverse cancer types. Although this issue has been addressed by previous studies 5,23 , most of them have investigated histological morphological features at the slide-level 5 and spot-level 23 , rather than single-nucleus-level. In this study, high-resolution WSIs of 8 cancer types, including BRCA ( n =1978 slides), COAD ( n =451 slides), OV ( n =106 slides), PRAD ( n =403 slides), PAAD ( n =183 slides), LIHC ( n =365 slides), KIRC ( n =513 slides) and LUSC ( n =478 slides) were collected from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database, where a total of 10,000 tiles for each cancer type were randomly down-sampled from original WSIs for downstream analysis. ## Discussion

Knowledge of cell spatial organization in tissues could enhance our understanding of the complex architecture of multicellular organisms 3 . In this study, we introduce HistoCell, a weakly-supervised modelfor super-resolution cell spatial pro fi le inference from histology images, including cell types and states of individual cells and spatial associations among diverse cells. Moreover, we also explored the potential utility of hierarchical cellular pro fi les in diverse clinical contexts. The key step of HistoCell is the systematic decoupling of the associations between macro-level histological morphologies and micro-level cellular features from ST data in a weakly-supervised manner. Benchmark analysis demonstrated that compared with other state-of-the-art weakly-supervised methods, HistoCell achieves the highest accuracy across various cancer types, even surpassing the fully-supervised ones. It was also revealed that HistoCell exhibited high robustness to the size of pre-trained data, the resolution of histology images and the deconvolution results obtained with different methods. Furthermore, we evaluated the performance of HistoCell across four applications, including ST data deconvolution, subtle tissue region annotation, clinically relevant spatial cellular features discovery and image-based cellular marker identi fi cation. Notably, HistoCell was applied to identify cell populations and their spatial organization indicative of the malignant transformation of gastric premalignant lesions from the gastric scRNA-seq pro fi le, which was validated in independent longitudinal cohorts. In summary, our proof-of-concept study provides a versatile tool for deciphering cellular pro fi les from histology images without annotations, and systematically characterizing cell spatial organization features with respect to clinical outcomes.

Recent studies have demonstrated the critical role of morphological characteristics in histology images for deciphering molecular and cellular information within ST data 22,45 . In this context, certain AI models have been proposed to be trained using ST data to capture the associations between transcriptional heterogeneity and cellular profi les, and then used in image-only cohorts. However, existing histology image analysis models based on ST data mainly focused on spot-level cell compositions and thus hinder the inference of speci fi c locations for individual cells. In this study, our model enables accurate prediction of single-nucleus-level super-resolution cell spatial pro fi les for histology images by integrating morphological and spatial features of individual nuclei, and introducing hierarchical structures on the predicted cell labels. In addition, it was demonstrated that HistoCell has strong generalizability. It could achieve considerable prediction performance in the case of a small training dataset (even with a single ST sample) since it has learned morphological characteristics and cell proportions within individual spots. Moreover, given that the predicted cellular labels are derived from spot-level cellular deconvolution results instead of manual annotations, the performance of this model can also be transferred to other types of spatial omics data, including spatial genomics and proteomics data. Speci fi cally, the recently developed 10× Genomics HD technology, which offers highquality ST data with in-situ sequencing information could also be used as training data for HistoCell to enhance its performance. Last but not least, the HistoCell models were pre-trained separately for different cancer types in our study to address the inter-tumor cellular heterogeneities, consistent with our observation that the cancer-speci fi c pretrained models showed superior performance than the single cancer-conserved one. Overall, our study might address the issue of uncovering the associations between morphological and cellular features.

It has been shown that cell spatial organization and tissue architecture offer critical insights into cancer biology and clinical outcomes 3,13,25 . Despite providing unpreceded high-resolution spatial information, ST technologies have not been utilized in large-scale clinical studies due to their high cost. In contrast, WSIs generated routinely in clinical are much easier and less expensive to obtain 14 , making it pivotal to infer super-resolution cell spatial pro fi les solely from WSIs. Traditionally, fully-supervised learning models have been adopted to predict clinically relevant histological morphological features. These studies remain signi fi cant barriers to clinical practice because they are usually limited by the use of cell information annotated by experts and the model interpretability 63 . Our research provides a paradigm for discovering associations between spatial cellular morphological features and clinical outcomes. Through our pretrained model on the small size of ST data, we can predict superresolution cell pro fi les on a large scale of WSIs without any pathological annotations and uncover their associations with clinical outcomes, enabling de novo discovery of interpretable clinically relevant morphological features. In addition, our studies have systematically deciphered histology images across diverse cancer types, providing a valuable data resource for digital cancer pathology. More importantly, our model can also link pathological morphology information with drug response, which might be of great value for discovering pathomorphological biomarkers related to drug ef fi cacy, and provide an approach for the development of network pharmacology AI methods and analysis platform (UNIQ system) that integrate pathological image information 64 . Collectively, we fi rmly believe that this model can provide a bridge between small-size molecular omics data and large imaging clinical cohorts, and fi ll the gap between cell spatial organization and clinical outcomes with the help of histology images.

We should demonstrate that several limitations still exist for HistoCell. Despite its high robustness, our model is in fl uenced by prerequisite cell segmentation from histology images, during which invalid segmentation might introduce noise to our analysis. Moreover, considering that the topological analysis of HistoCell is limited to cells within single spots, more topological associations among cells across ST spots could be introduced in terms of model construction. In parallel, the performance of our model would be enhanced with training on higher-resolution ST data, including single-cell scale ST data derived from 10× Genomics HD technology. Finally, these cell spatial features, which are indicative of cancer clinical outcomes inferred by our study should be evaluated in more large cohorts, including prospective clinical trials. Nonetheless, we believe that the versatile ability of HistoCell to decipher the super-resolution cell spatial pro fi le from histology image will empower discoveries in spatial biology and clinical medicine.

## Data collection and pre-processing

Spatial transcriptomic data . In this study, to perform model pretraining and internal validation, a series of spatial transcriptomics (ST) datasets co-registering high-resolution histology images were collected from publicly available data resources (Supplementary Table 1). As a result, a total of 99 samples across 9 cancer types, along with 152,168 spots were collected, of which 25 samples were from formalin fi xed paraf fi n-embedded (FFPE) tissues with other 74 samples from frozen tissues. In addition, to train the model related to gastric tumorigenesis, we performed spatial transcriptomic sequencing (10× Genomics) on two gastric early-malignant FFPE samples with coexisting low-grade dysplasia foci (GSE287979), resulting in a gastric cancer-related ST dataset co-registering with high-resolution pathology images. Spots with low gene expression level (total counts &lt;3) were removed and the raw expression matrix was fi rst normalized and log-transformed before deconvolution. The details of collected spotlevel ST data were summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Additionally, to evaluate the performance of HistoCell in singlenucleus-level cell type prediction and deconvolution enhancement of independent ST data, we also collected the breast cancer ST data derived from the 10× Xenium platform under accession code GSE243280, which included both co-registered high resolution histology images and single-cell level expression data. Through the Xenium analyzer, a spatial transcriptomic map was constructed across the entire tissue section at single-cell resolution by decoding optical features of target genes. In total, we detected 167,780 cells in replication 1 and 118,752 cells in replication 2, both of which contain 313 sequenced genes. The type for each single-cell was annotated according to the marker genes.

scRNA-seqdata . To perform cell type deconvolution analysis for spotlevel ST data in the process of model pre-training, a total of 9 scRNAseq datasets matched with ST data were collected as reference. For each dataset, we derived the expression matrix as the input of deconvolution analysis and collected the annotated cell types in corresponding studies. Similarly, we also performed expression normalization and removed cells with total expression counts lower than 3. The details of collected scRNA-seq datasets were summarized in Supplementary Table 2.

Histology image data . In this work, to perform model evaluation and application analysis, high-resolution histology images, mainly in the form of whole slide images (WSI), were collected from diverse sources, including publicly available image data resources (TCGA, PanNuke, and Camelyon16) and images co-registered in ST data (10× Visium and 10× Xenium). Generally, these histology images from publicly available image data resources could be classi fi ed as three categories: images with slide-level labels/phenotypes (TCGA), images with tissue region annotations (Camelyon16) and images with single-nucleus-level manual annotations (PanNuke). For TCGA dataset, we collected histology images and corresponding clinical outcomes of 8 cancer types (BRCA, COAD, OVC, PRAD, PDAC, LIHC, KIRC and LUSC) that HistoCell involved, to perform pan-cancer cell nuclei morphological feature analysis and clinically relevant spatial organization indicators (SOIs) identi fi cation. Camelyon16 is a lymph node histology image dataset containing manual annotations of each in fi ltrated breast tumor region. In this regard, we collected 100 metastatic and 100 normal samples respectively from the training set to perform tissue architecture annotation and metastasis-related SOIs identi fi cation. As for the PanNuke dataset, 7961 tiles with the size of 256 × 256 pixels were collected from 19 tissue types, all of which were manually annotated with singlenucleus-level cell types, including neoplastic cells, in fl ammatory, connective/soft tissue cells, dead cells, epithelial, and background. In this study, we utilized 5002 tiles across 9 cancer types as the external validation dataset for model evaluation.

As the fi rst step of image data pre-processing, we performed tile/ patch analysis with different strategies in terms of data sources. For histology images without morphological annotations, the CLAM 5 toolbox was used due to its convenience and ef fi ciency in histology images preprocessing. ## Image feature extraction

As the pivotal step, HistoCell should extract both embedded morphological features and topological features of pathology images from the perspective of cell instances, followed by hierarchically encoding these features with the unidirectional long short-term memory (LSTM) module. The detailed procedure of image feature extraction was demonstrated below.

Embeddedmorphologicalfeatureextraction . After patching and cell segmentation, we adopted the ResNet-18 model pre-trained on ImageNet 65 to extract the features of the tissue tiles and cell image tokens since ResNet-18 could avoid over fi tting and high training cost. In detail, we converted each image tile into a 512-dimensional vector b 2 R F . As for the segmented cell image tokens, we fi rst resized them to patches with the size of 30×30 pixels for batch computing and extracted features with the same ResNet-18 model. As the resizing process ignores the size information of each cell, we additionally encode the cell size information u 2 R F 0 (e.g. the major and minor axis length) and fuse it with the cell embedded morphological features v 2 R F through a linear layer to obtain the embedded morphological features of single nucleus c 2 R F .

## Cell hierarchical information annotation

In this study, we conducted deconvolution analysis on the spatial expression data to acquire spot-level cell hierarchical information, including cell compartments, cell types and cell states, as weak supervision labels for the development of HistoCell models.

As for cell type deconvolution, the proportions of each cell type within individual spots were obtained by averaging the deconvoluted proportions from four state-of-the-art deconvolution methods with default parameters, including CARD 66 , RCTD 32 , Tangram 33 and POLARIS 24 , with the matched scRNA-seq data as reference (Supplementary Table 2). Furthermore, three main cell compartments, including epithelial ( ' Epi ' ), tumor microenvironment ( ' TME ' ) and mixture ones ( ' Mix ' ) were annotated, where spots with proportions of epithelial cells higher than 75% were annotated as Epi spots, as well as those with proportions of immune cells higher than 75% as TME spots, and the other spots as Mix spots. As for cell state deconvolution, the framework of Ecotyper 34 was used. Here, Cibersortx 67 was fi rstly used to deconvolute the spatial transcriptomics data and infer gene expression pro fi les (GEPs) for each cell type. Consequently, the Nonnegative Matrix Factorization (NMF) was applied to these GEPs and every single coef fi cient matrix of the NMF result was scaled to present the proportion of cell states in a speci fi c cell type. Speci fi cally, we deciphered the cell states for epithelial cells and adopted the 6 prede fi ned cell states in Ecotyper, including the basal-like, normal-enriched, pro-angiogenic (also known as EMT), pro-in fl ammatory, metabolic and other common epithelial. Collectively, the cell hierarchical information has been inferred and annotated for each ST spot according to the deconvolution.

Training strategy . Given the hierarchical relationships between cell types and states, we employed a two-stage training strategy that the model was fi rstly trained for the single-nucleus-level cell type prediction, followed by single-nucleus cell state prediction. In detail, we trained the model for 50 epochs with the supervision of cell compartment loss and KL divergence loss ( L ce + L KL ). Then, the model was trained to predict the cell states for another 50 epochs with the supervision of overall loss L ( L ce + L KL + β L con ; β =0 : 1). During the training procedure, the model parameters were updated via the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10 -4 . In our study, to highlight the rationality of our two-stage training strategy, we also compared the performance of the model using the two-stage training strategy with that of the model using the one-stage training strategy that hierarchical cell information was predicted directly with the supervision of overall loss L (Supplementary Fig. 3d).

Cross-validation analysis . To assess the rationality and robustness of the methodological framework of HistoCell, two different crossvalidation methods, leave-one-out and 10-fold validation, were fi rstly employed on the ST data we collected across nine cancer types (Supplementary Tables 1 and 3). The leave-one-out validation was conducted for cancer types with more than one ST sample ( n &gt;1 sample, BRCA, LIHC, PRAD, KIRC, PAAD and STAD), which was selected primarily due to the relatively small size of ST samples for each cancer type and potential inter-sample variations. For each round, spots from one ST sample were selected as the validation data while those from the other ST samples as the training data (detailed as ' training strategy ' ). Alternatively, the 10-fold cross-validation was conducted for cancer types with only one ST sample ( n =1 sample, COAD, LUSC and OV). For each round, the spots from the single ST sample were randomly split into 10 folds, from which 9 folds were utilized as the training data while the other one fold was for model validation. For each round of the two cross-validation methods, the benchmark metric of the trained model was determined by the Pearson correlation coef fi cient (PCC) between the spot-level cell proportions predicted solely based on pathology images and those derived from the original deconvolution analysis of the expression data. Here, the predicted single-nucleus-level cells in each spot were clumped to mimic spot-level cell proportions. The detailed numbers of total ST samples, as well as each round of training and internal validation data across nine cancer types were detailed in Supplementary Table 3. In addition, for each round of cross-validation, the comparative model, POLARIS 24 , was established with default parameters on the training data same as HistoCell, and then used to predict spot-level cell proportions for inter-validation spots solely based on the pathology images. Here, the PCCs between the POLORIS-image-inferred cell proportions and originally deconvoluted ones were also used as the benchmark metric.

Final model selection . The fi nal pre-trained models were separately established for nine cancer types, with combining all ST samples for each cancer type as training data and adopting the two-stage training strategy as used in cross-validation analysis (detailed as ' training strategy ' ). These fi nal pre-trained models were furtherly used for validation analysis on external datasets and downstream application experiments.

## Model evaluation: benchmark analysis on external datasets

To furtherly evaluate the generalization ability of HistoCell, we employed the fi nal pre-trained models to predict cell types at the single-nucleus-level based on histology images from two external datasets with de fi nitive cellular annotations, including the 10× Xenium breast cancer ST dataset 68 and the PanNuke dataset 41 . Here, the 10× Xenium breast cancer ST dataset provided the in-situ sequencing information as cellular annotations while the PanNuke dataset provide single-nucleus-level manual annotations (tumor, immune and stromal cells).

For the 10× Xenium ST dataset, the fi nal pre-trained model for BRCA was selected since the dataset only included breast cancer samples. In this context, a total of seven cell types were predicted by our model solely based on the pathology images from the dataset, including cancer epithelial cells, normal epithelial cells, myeloid cells, T cells, B cells, stromal cells and endothelial cells, and the one-versus-rest AUC value was used as the benchmark metric for each cell type.

For the PanNuke dataset, all the nine pre-trained models were involved, with each applied on the pathology images of corresponding tumors, as shown in Supplementary Table 4. Given that the comparative model HoVerNet was established on the PanNuke dataset and could not be trained on the ST data, we re-trained our models on the same training data as used in HoVerNet to perform benchmark analysis. According to the training procedure of HoVerNet, we split the pathology image tiles of each cancer type from the PanNuke dataset into three folds, of which two were used as the training data and the remaining one as the validation data. It should be noted that the HistoCell model for each cancer type was re-trained with the same frameworks of image feature extraction and model construction (detailed as ' Image feature extraction ' and ' Pre-trained model construction ' ). As a result, three cell types (tumor cells, immune cells and stromal cells) were predicted by our re-trained models on the validation data for each cancer type and the one-versus-rest AUC value was also used as the benchmark metric for each cell type.

To numerically evaluate the performance of our model, one weakly-supervised model, Multi-instance Learning (MIL)-based model, and two fully-supervised models, HoVerNet [10] and MaskRCNN [44] , were introduced as comparison models on the two external datasets. Here, HoVerNet and MaskRCNN were developed with training directly on annotated histology images from PanNuke with default parameters. The MIL-based model was developed with training on the cell compositions within multiple instances of ST data, consistent with those in HistoCell. Speci fi cally, the MIL method regarded the embedded morphological features of single nuclei with 512 dimensions C 2 R N × F extracted by ResNet-18 as the instances and aggregated the instancelevel features into the bag-level feature  c 2 R F through maximization.

<!-- formula-not-decoded -->

Following that, a linear layer with an input size of 512 and an output size of the number of cell type was employed to predict the cell compositions. The KL divergence between the predicted and deconvolution cell compositions were adopted as the loss function and we employed Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10 -4 to update the parameters of MIL model. During inference, we employed the pretrained linear layer to directly predict the cell types based on the embedded morphological features of individual nuclei. As a result, a total of nine MIL-based models were separately constructed for the nine cancer types that HistoCell involved.

For the 10× Xenium ST dataset, the MIL-based model pre-trained for BRCA was selected to predict seven cell types same as the one in the HistoCell pre-trained model, including cancer epithelial cells, normal epithelial cells, myeloid cells, T cells, B cells, stromal cells and endothelial cells. Alternatively, the HoVerNet and MaskRCNN models were employed here to predict three previously annotated cell types, including cancer cells, immune cells and stromal cells. For the PanNuke dataset, all the three comparative models were developed to predict the cancer, immune and stromal cells. For all of the three comparison models, the one-versus-rest AUC value was used as the benchmark metric for each cell type.

## Model evaluation: ablation analysis

In this study, in order to evaluate the effect and relative value of each model component in the overall prediction task, we performed ablation analysis with removing and replacing individual model components of HistoCell.

Ablation analysis on removing individual model components of HistoCell . We performed ablation analysis by comparing the singlenucleus-level cell type prediction performance of the CNN-only model, the CNN+GAT model, the CNN+LSTM model and the CNN+GAT+ LSTM (i.e. HistoCell model) on the PanNuke dataset. All these four ablation models employed the features extracted by ResNet-18 as the initial input. The CNN-only model predicted the single-nucleus-level cell types directly using the linear layer with the 512-dimentional embedded morphological features as input. The CNN + GAT model used an additional GAT module to obtain the 512-dimentional topological features among cell nuclei and then predicted the single-nucleuslevel cell types, while the CNN + LSTM model used an additional LSTM model to extract the tile-level morphological feature within individual spots and predicted the cell types with both the 512-dimentional tilelevel feature and the 512-dimentional cell morphological features extracted by the aforementioned ResNet-18. The detailed model architectures in ablation analysis were demonstrated in Supplementary Fig. 11. In this context, we trained each ablation model on the PanNuke dataset with same strategy described in HistoCell ( ' Pre-trained model construction: training strategy ' ), and the one-versus-rest AUC value was used as the benchmark metric for each cell type.

Ablation analysis on replacing individual model components of HistoCell . Besides the ablation study for the effects of removing each model component of HistoCell, we furtherly assessed the effects of model selection in each component on the fi nal results. All the replaced modules had the same input and output dimensions as the original ones within HistoCell. In the nuclei morphological feature embedding module, encoders with larger parameters including ResNet-50 and DenseNet-121 were selected for comparison. Both ResNet-50 and DenseNet-121 were used to extract the features with 512 dimensions, which is the same with ResNet-18. In the topological feature extraction module, we selected other two graph neural network learning methods, detailed as GCN and GraphSAGE. Same as GAT, they took the embedded morphological features with 512 dimensions as input and provided the features with 512 dimensions fusing the topological features as output. In terms of the hierarchical encoding module, we used the RNN with single hidden layer to replace the LSTM for ablation analysis. The RNN took the same input data and output hierarchical cellular information as the LSTM. In this context, the models with replaced modules were trained on the PanNuke dataset with same strategy described in HistoCell ( ' Pre-trained model construction: training strategy ' ) and the one-versus-rest AUC value was also used as the benchmark metric for each cell type.

## Model application 2: tissue architecture annotation

In our study, the single-nucleus-level cell type annotation provided by HistoCell enabled the accurate super resolution tissue architecture annotation on the independent ST data.

As for the single-nucleus-level region annotation, the pre-trained model for BRCA was employed on both HER2ST and Camelyon16 datasets. We fi rstly divided pathology image tiles into individual grids (5 µ m×5 µ m), where the single-nucleus-level cellular distribution was inferred for each grid by the pre-trained model for BRCA. Then, for each grid, one of the four tissue types, including the cancer epithelial, normal epithelial, stromal and immune regions, was annotated based on the most enriched cell type. It is worth mentioned that the 5 µ m×5 µ mgrid is comparable to the size of single nucleus. As for the grids without any nucleus, the k Nearest Neighborhood (KNN, k =7) algorithm was applied, where the tissue type of each grid was annotated based on the most dominate tissue type among spatially neighborhood grids. Collectively, we provided single-nucleus-level accurate tissue architecture annotation through HistoCell.

As for the spot-level tissue annotation, a two-step strategy of spatial clustering was adopted on the HER2ST dataset. Firstly, the spatial clustering algorithm, GraphST, was applied to identify the baseline clusters for each ST sample based on the corresponding spatial expression data with default parameters. Then, these baseline clusters were re fi ned according to the cell pro fi les inferred by the HistoCell pretrained model for BRCA based on the corresponding pathology images. Here, within each baseline cluster, we modeled the HistoCell-inferred cell proportions across spots as the normal distribution with standard deviation ( σ =1) for individual cell types, and furtherly identi fi ed the additional clusters consisting of spots whose proportion of any cell type was outside the 95% con fi dence interval (3 σ ).

## Model application 3: pan-cancer cell morphological feature analysis

In order to systematically dissect cancer cell morphological features across diverse organs, we conducted pan-cancer analysis on the basis of HistoCell-inferred cell spatial pro fi les from histology images from TCGA. In total, 8 cancer types, including BRCA, COAD, OVC, PRAD, PDAC, LIHC, KIRC and LUSC, were included in our analysis. In detail, we fi rst randomly down-sampled a total of 10,000 pathology image tiles for each cancer type and extracted the morphological features for each single nucleus with the HistoCell encoder that was pre-trained on corresponding ST data (Supplementary Table 3). Focusing on the cancer epithelial cells, we extracted the 512-dimensional embedded morphological features for each cell along with the corresponding cell states. Tovisualize the global distribution of the pan-cancer cellular embedded morphological features, we used UMAP for dimension reduction. More than 1.5 million cells were detected and processed here in total.

## Model application 4: clinically-relevant SOIs identi fi cation

Cell spatial network construction . To dissect the cell spatial organization of histology images in a global manner, we constructed slide-level cell spatial networks based on the single-nucleus-level cell pro fi les predicted by HistoCell. Here, network nodes are individual cell instances while edges refer to the spatial correlations between cell instances. Given the substantial number of cell instances present in a single whole slide image (WSI), a spot-based k nearest neighborhood strategy was adopted. For each WSI, we fi rstly cut it into spots with the size of 256 × 256 pixels. Then, for each cell instance, only the spot that this instance located at and its surrounding spots were focused, where only the spatial correlations between this cell instance and its k -nearest neighbors ( k =7) with same cell type were remained. Thus, we could transform a WSI into a cell spatial network consisting of spatially closely connected cell clusters, from which the spatial information, including cluster centroid, cell number and cell dominated area, could be derived. Here, three main cell types were involved, including cancer cells, immune cells and stromal cells. The python package networkx was employed to conduct network analysis.

Spatial organization indicators identi fi cation . Inspired by the previous studies 56 , lists of spatial organization indicators (SOIs) were de fi ned based on the constructed cell spatial network, including the intra-population SOIs (cell proportions, densities and spatial distributions of individual cell population) and the inter-population SOIs (spatial distributions between different cell populations), as listed in the Supplementary Data 1.

Prognosis analysis . We assessed the prognostic value of these SOIs by correlating with overall survival information documented in TCGA. Firstly, we employed the pre-trained HistoCell models for BRCA, LIHC, andPAADtopredictthesingle-nucleus-level cell types on TCGA-BRCA, TCGA-LIHC and TCGA-PAAD datasets, respectively. Following that, we identi fi ed the SOIs and strati fi ed the patients as high-risk and low-risk ones according to the median value of each SOI. Survival curves were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method, log-rank and univariate tests with ' KaplanMeierFitter ' package. In order to make the analysis results more convincing, fi ve-fold cross validation was additional conducted.

Model application 5: image-based cellular marker identi fi cation Construction of the HistoCell model for STAD . To infer the spatial pro fi les of diverse cell populations involved in gastric tumorigenesis, we trained the HistoCell model, denoted as the pre-trained model for STAD, on the ST data of gastric early-malignant FFPE samples with coexisting premalignant foci, by following the aforementioned analysis protocols of ' Pre-trained model construction ' . Here, the scRNA-seq pro fi le underlying gastric premalignant and early malignant lesions that established in our previous study 61 (GSE134520) was used as reference data of ST data deconvolution analysis.

Construction of longitudinal cohorts of gastric LGD patients . To evaluate the application value of HistoCell in identifying cell populations indicative of malignant transformation for gastric low-grade dysplasia (LGD), two independent retrospective longitudinal cohorts (Cohort 1, 94 patients, training data; Cohort 2, 169 patients, testing data) were established, where patients with baseline LGD lesions were enrolled with a long surveillance period (details in Supplementary Table 6). The baseline clinicopathological parameters (sex, gender, H.p infection) and conventional phenotype information related to traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), including tongue colors and pulse patterns, were collected from these patients. The patients of Cohort 1 were enrolled from the Pathology Departments of the First Af fi liated Hospital of WannanMedical College and Wangjing Hospital of CACMS, China. The patients of Cohort 2 were enrolled from the Pathology Departments of Peking Union Medical College Hospital and ChinaJapan Friendship Hospital, China. For each cohort, we collected FFPE samples from LGD patients with more than twice surveillance endoscopy records, with excluding those patients with surveillance intervals less than 3 months. No statistical methods were used to predetermine group size. For this study, the follow-up interval was calculated from the date of the endoscopic procedure on which the sample was originally obtained to the date of baseline diagnosis of high-grade dysplasia or gastric cancer (progressors) or censored date of last surveillance biopsy or fi rst ablation procedure (non-progressors). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at all hospitals involved, while informed consent was waived by the IRBs because of the retrospective nature. For all enrolled patients, histologic examinations were carefully performed according to the updated Sydney System by at least two experienced pathologists (Z.P and Z.W.X) from different institutions, who were blinded to original diagnosis, progression status, and clinical endpoint results. Degrees of dysplasia were diagnosed according to the revised Vienna classi fi cation 69 and classi fi cation of carcinomas were performed according to the WHO classi fi cation of tumors. Cancer in the T0 stage (carcinoma in situ) was de fi ned as the early gastric cancer (EGC) in this study according to the 7th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM stage system 70 .

For each FFPE sample, 3μ m-thick section was cut and stained with H&amp;Eperstandard protocol. These slides were then scanned at 40x using a KF-PRO-120 scanner to derive high-resolution histology images (WSIs).

Integrative analysis of image-based spatial organization and clinical indicators . It has been shown that clinical information derived from both western and traditional chinese medicine indicated the risk of malignant gastric transformation. To furtherly improved the prediction performance, we utilized logistic regression to integrate the HistoCell-derived SOI and clinical indicators. In detail, the SOI of KLK10 + cell aggregation density, the western clinical indicators including age, sex and helicobator infection, and the TCM phenotypes were adopted for integration. We regarded the probabilities given by logistic regression model as the values of integrative indicator and utilized it for risk strati fi cation.
